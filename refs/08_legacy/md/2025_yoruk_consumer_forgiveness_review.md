### Abstract 

Consumer forgiveness represents a compelling phenomenon for marketers, as understanding and leveraging forgiveness allows repairing consumer brand relationships. Marketing scholars began to pay closer attention to consumer forgiveness in the last decade with attempts to better understand and analyze this construct. This literature review on consumer forgiveness seeks to enrich the discourse on this phenomenon through a comprehensive systematic review of 89 articles. Through this literature review, we synthesize the findings of 89 articles to (1) understand how involved agents (e.g., firms/brands, humans, and autonomous technologies) influence the consumer forgiveness process, (2) develop a theoretical integrative framework delineating salient psychological mechanisms of the consumer forgiveness process, and (3) propose a future research agenda on consumer forgiveness.

### 1 | INTRODUCTION 

Consumer forgiveness is increasingly viewed as a critical mechanism for repairing consumer‐brand relationships after negative marketing events such as product‐harm crisis, service failures, and brand transgressions (Khamitov et al., 2020). Consumer forgiveness is a significant concept for businesses for its potential as a strategic tool to restore and repair trust and loyalty among customers affected by negative marketing events (Xie & Peng, 2009). As companies face the challenges of maintaining customer satisfaction and loyalty in a turbulent environment, understanding how to effectively facilitate forgiveness has become essential (Kim et al., 2023; Tsarenko & Tojib, 2015). Therefore, the concept of consumer forgiveness has recently garnered significant interest among marketing scholars, particularly due to a rise in negative marketing events and the growing necessity for obtaining forgiveness from consumers. 

The idea of forgiveness first appeared in theology research and later has been extensively studied in psychology, where experts have 

investigated how forgiving self and others can help heal and repair relationships. In the marketing domain, the interest in comprehending the dynamics of forgiveness is driven by the recognition that consumer forgiveness has profound consequences on the longevity of consumer‐brand relationships amid negative marketing events. For example, scholars in service marketing research examine forgiveness as the unique way to mend and maintain the bond between consumers and brands after service failures (Kim & Ho, Tan, et al., 2023). 

Although consumer forgiveness is getting increased scholarly attention in the marketing literature, there still lacks a systematic understanding of the consumer forgiveness processes and the resulting implications for marketers and practitioners. Furthermore, the introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) and autonomous technologies into businesses adds another layer of complexity to the consumer forgiveness phenomenon where such technologies either contribute to the wrongdoings and/or play a role in the recovery after related negative marketing events (Agnihotri & Bhattacharya, 2024; Arikan et al., 2023), influencing the consumer forgiveness processes.

Prior research has examined different aspects of forgiveness, such as its antecedents, consequences, and other underlying psychological processes such as mediators and moderators (Kim & Ho, Tan, et al., 2023; Özdemir, 2023). Witnessing the increasing importance of consumer forgiveness in marketing literature, two systematic literature review studies (e.g., Kim & Ho, Tan, et al., 2023; Özdemir, 2023) have been conducted to synthesize the consumer forgiveness literature. In particular, Kim, and Ho, Tan, et al. (2023) have summarized the literature on forgiveness within the context of service failures (without delving into product failure or brand transgression literatures). In parallel, Özdemir (2023) has conducted a systematic overview of the larger consumer forgiveness literature providing a descriptive summary of the literature by year and journal, theoretical background, methodological trends, and thematic issues (e.g., antecedents, consequences, mediators, and moderators). Özdemir (2023) lamented that while the number of research paper on consumer forgiveness is increasing, at least a fourth of the studies “lack the utilization of any theoretical framework when providing the conceptual framework” (Özdemir, 2023, p. 1252). Furthermore, Kim, and Ho, Tan, et al. (2023) called for research that integrates theoretical perspectives into a unique framework to explain consumer forgiveness from different perspectives. We concur with these comments and respond to them with our attempt to develop a systematic understanding of the consumer forgiveness literature (see Table 1). For this purpose, in this paper we employ a deep exploration of a theory‐driven integrative framework delineating salient psychological mechanisms of the consumer forgiveness process. We then integrate and synthesize our findings to propose a new framework for consumer forgiveness that is of paramount importance and will steer the future research on consumer forgiveness. In our literature review, we encompass all consumer forgiveness research conducted in the contexts of service failure, product failure and brand transgressions contexts. Additionally, we integrate findings of qualitative research into our synthesis. 

Against this backdrop, this systematic literature review aims to (1) understand how involved agents (e.g., firms/brands, humans, and autonomous technologies) influence the consumer forgiveness process, (2) develop a theory‐driven integrative framework delineating salient psychological mechanisms of the consumer forgiveness process, and (3) propose future research avenues on consumer forgiveness. This paper not only advances the growing body of knowledge of consumer forgiveness but also aids marketers and 

practitioners with guidelines on effectively repairing customer relationships amid negative marketing events. 

The paper is structured as follows. The methodology section describes the literature search and identification processes. The analysis and synthesis section provides an in‐depth review and synthesis of the theories used in explaining consumer forgiveness, definitions of consumer forgiveness, contexts in which consumer forgiveness has been examined, and mediating and moderating variables that play a role in the consumer forgiveness process. This section serves to identify how involved agents (e.g., firms/brands, humans, and autonomous technologies) influence the consumer forgiveness process and informs the proposed framework. The subsequent results section introduces the proposed integrative framework of consumer forgiveness, where consumer forgiveness is portrayed as a coping process where salient theoretical lenses are consolidated in different stages of the process (e.g., attribution theory and justice theory). Finally, discussion of the implications for research and practice, future research directions, and limitations are provided.

### 3.1 | Theories 

The primary research objective of this literature review study is to develop a theory‐driven integrative framework delineating salient psychological mechanisms of the consumer forgiveness process. For this purpose, we review the consumer forgiveness literature and identify the predominant theoretical frameworks used to examine the consumer forgiveness process. Table 2 summarizes the theories adopted in the consumer forgiveness literature and their frequencies of use. As seen in Table 2, the most frequently used theories to understand consumer forgiveness behavior have been (1) attribution theory, (2) coping theory, and (3) justice theory. In this paper we focus on the three most frequently used theories and work with an amalgamation of them to explain consumer forgiveness. Moreover, coping theory, attribution theory, and justice theory demonstrate complementariness to each other in depicting a holistic view of the consumer forgiveness process. We build primarily on these three groups of theories in constructing an integrative framework of consumer forgiveness as a coping process where the attributions of the causes of negative marketing events and the perceived justices in the recovery efforts play a key role in influencing consumer forgiveness and related outcomes. Whenever appropriate, concepts identified from other theoretical perspectives and relevant to the consumer forgiveness coping process are incorporated into the integrative framework to provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon. 

### 3.1.1 | Coping theory 

Coping theory offers a valuable framework for understanding how individuals navigate stressful, adverse, and challenging situations. It posits that individuals' perceptions and appraisals of stressors are critical in determining their coping responses (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). The coping process is fundamentally based on two key appraisals: primary appraisal, which involves assessing whether a situation poses a threat, challenge, or harm, and secondary appraisal, which involves evaluating the resources available to manage the situation. 

In the context of consumer forgiveness, coping theory is particularly relevant for explaining how individuals respond to negative marketing events and has been extensively adopted in the literature (Bath & Bawa, 2020; Harrison‐Walker, 2019; Kaur et al., 2022; Lin & Chou, 2022; Riaz & Khan, 2016; Salagrama et al., 2021; Tsarenko & Rooslani Tojib, 2011; Yang & Hu, 2021; Zourrig et al., 2009). During the primary appraisal stage, consumers assess 

the negative marketing event to determine its significance, specifically whether it threatens their sense of fairness or harms their relationship with the brand. This appraisal is not merely transactional but is also deeply emotional, triggering a range of negative emotions such as disappointment, anger, and betrayal. The disruption to the transactional exchange and the resulting emotional distress create conditions in which consumers may consider forgiveness as a coping mechanism. 

The secondary appraisal stage involves an evaluation of the resources available to cope with the situation. Consumers assess whether they possess sufficient emotional resilience, prior experience, or social support to manage the disruption and accompanying negative emotions. If they perceive that they have adequate resources to address these challenges, they may choose to engage in forgiveness as a deliberate coping strategy. Additionally, if consumers perceive that the offending party has made significant reparative efforts and demonstrated sincere remorse, they are more inclined to forgive. Such forgiveness helps reestablish relational balance and allows consumers to continue engaging in the transactional relationship without the burden of unresolved negative feelings (Tsarenko & Rooslani Tojib, 2011; Tsarenko & Tojib, 2012). 

Consumer forgiveness is not limited to specific types of negative marketing events, such as product‐harm crises, service failures, or brand transgressions. Each of these events may be perceived by consumers as either a challenge or a harm, thereby triggering the primary appraisal process. For example, product‐harm crises may pose a direct threat to consumers' health and safety, resulting in significant emotional distress. Service failures may violate consumers' expectations of reliability and trustworthiness, leading to feelings of frustration and disappointment. Brand transgressions, such as ethical breaches or misleading advertising, may damage consumers' sense of integrity and fairness, causing feelings of betrayal and anger. Despite the variations in the nature of these events, they all challenge consumers' expectations and can be perceived as harmful to their physical, emotional, or psychological well‐being. These perceived challenges or harms activate the coping process, prompting consumers to engage in primary and secondary appraisals to manage their emotional responses and decide on appropriate actions as an individual.

Individuals can attribute the cause of events or behaviors to either internal (personal) or external (situational) factors. Such attributions are based on the observed covariation of a cause with an effect across time, place, and different situations. ### 3.1.3 | Justice theory 

Justice theory is a branch of philosophy that explores the concept of justice and how it can be applied to create a fair and equitable society. It aims to explain how individuals perceive justice and provide principles for determining what is just or unjust in different contexts (Greenberg, 1990). Justice is typically defined as the quality of being fair, reasonable, and impartial (Greenberg, 1990). The effect of perceived justice has been highlighted to have crucial roles specifically in the recovery stage after negative marketing events (the secondary appraisal) and justice is examined to influence consumer forgiveness outcomes. Commonly, consumers need the restoration of justice after transgressions. When individuals perceive that the transgressors' actions as unfair or unjust (e.g., due to intent or negligence), they are less inclined to forgive (Babin et al., 2021). Interactional justice, distributive justice, and procedural justice are three subdimensions of justice theory. Interactional justice focuses on whether people perceive the behavior of others, such as transgressors, as respectful, polite, and/or considerate. Consumers are more likely to forgive when they perceive that the wrongdoers treat them with respect, empathy, and honesty in recovery processes. In situations where the interaction is characterized by rudeness, insensitivity, or deception, forgiveness is less likely. Distributive justice is linked to consumers' perceptions of whether the wrongdoers have provided adequate compensation or restitution. When consumers perceive that the compensation for losses has been fair, they are more inclined to forgive (Wei et al., 2020). Finally, procedural justice is related to the processes employed by the wrongdoers in addressing negative events. When consumers perceive that the procedures for addressing the event are fair, open, and give them a voice, they are more likely to forgive (Muhammad & Mokhtar, 2017). Procedural injustice, such as secretive decision‐making or a lack of opportunity for consumers to express their grievances, hinders forgiveness. 

To conclude, coping, attribution, and justice theories have been extensively adopted in prior studies to explain consumer forgiveness amidst negative marketing events and provide solid theoretical foundations for developing the integrative framework of consumer forgiveness. Following the literature, we propose consumer forgiveness as a coping process where causal attributions of wrongdoings play a key role in primary appraisal and perceptions of justice shape the forgiving responses in secondary appraisal. 

### 3.2 | Consumer forgiveness conceptualization 

Marketing scholars draw upon various streams of research in psychology, particularly intrapersonal and interpersonal forgiveness, to conceptualize consumer forgiveness. This cross‐disciplinary borrowing has resulted in diverse definitions of consumer forgiveness within marketing literature. Table 3 summarizes the most commonly adopted consumer forgiveness definitions. However, this fragmented literature has not yielded a distinct, agreed‐upon definition of 

consumer forgiveness in marketing. Consequently, scholars have adopted an array of scales to measure this construct, reflecting the multidimensionality of forgiveness (see Appendix A). The lack of consensus on the definition and suitable measurement scales for consumer forgiveness poses a critical challenge in comprehending its role in consumer‐brand relationships. Therefore, we aim to provide a thorough understanding of this consumer forgiveness concept in this section. 

Research have explored motivational, emotional, cognitive, and behavioral components of consumer forgiveness (Babin et al., 2021; McCullough et al., 1998; Worthington & Scherer, 2004). Initial research depicted forgiveness purely as a motivational concept, where individuals who are willing to forgive, reduced their motivation for retaliation and estrangement, maintained conciliation, and fostered goodwill toward the offender (McCullough et al., 1997). As research expanded, McCullough et al. (1998) and Enright et al. (1998) proposed that besides motivation, there are emotional and cognitive components of forgiveness. The emotional component of forgiveness refers to the regulation of individuals' internal affective responses to a transgression and involves a deliberate process of replacing negative emotions with positive ones (Enright et al., 1998; Joireman et al., 2016; Strelan & Covic, 2006; Worthington & Scherer, 2004). The cognitive component of forgiveness refers to the extent to which the consumer attributes certain blame to the transgressor (Hall & Fincham, 2005; Hook et al., 2009). Lastly, it has been proposed that there is also a behavioral component to forgiveness. The behavioral component of forgiveness includes reducing rumination, or harmful behavior towards the transgressor and leads to a person behaving toward the transgressor in the same manner before the transgression (Worthington & Scherer, 2004). A summary of most adopted definitions of consumer forgiveness is provided in Table 3. 

Marketing scholars largely adopted the intrapersonal forgiveness definitions from the psychology literature to explain consumer forgiveness, with the exception of two studies that expanded these definitions to take the transactional nature of market exchanges into consideration. Notably, Tsarenko and Tojib (2015) advanced our understanding of consumer forgiveness within the marketing context via anchoring their study on an affective definition of forgiveness from the work of Enright et al. (1998) while addressing the affective differences between intrapersonal forgiveness and consumer forgiveness. Enright et al. (1998, p. 46) define forgiveness as “a willingness to abandon one's right to resentment, negative judgment, and indifferent behavior towards one who unjustly hurt us, while fostering the undeserved qualities of compassion, generosity, and even love towards him or her.” This definition focuses on the emotional transformation of the individual, where both negative emotions (like resentment) are released, and positive emotions (like compassion and love) are fostered. (1998)'s definition—fostering positive emotions like love and compassion— might not be fully applicable in a consumer context. ### 3.3 | Contexts 

Consumer forgiveness has been researched in the following contexts of negative marketing events: service failures (59 out of 89 papers), brand transgressions (13 out of 89 papers), and product‐harm crises (8 out of 89 papers). Service failures refer to a situation where service performance fails to meet consumer's expectations (Smith et al., 1999). Brand transgressions are characterized as violations of the implicit or explicit rules and norms concerning consumer‐brand relationship performance and evaluation (Aaker et al., 2004). Product‐harm crises refer to occasions where defective or dangerous products lead to widespread public concern and negative publicity (Dawar & Pillutla, 2000). 

Within the service failure context, three sub‐contexts have emerged: human‐human interactions, human‐brand interactions, and human‐autonomous agent interactions. It is interesting to observe that recent studies started examining service recovery effectiveness 

withing human‐autonomous agent interactions. For example, Cheng (2023) examines how the service robots' level of anthropomorphism influenced consumers' attributional process and then forgiveness of the concerned service failures; similarly, Arikan et al. (2023) identify the impacts of human‐likeness and humanness on responsibility attribution processes and the resulting effects on consumer forgiveness. Leveraging the computers are social actors' theory, Agnihotri and Bhattacharya (2024) study how the traits of AI‐based chatbots led to higher level of perceived trustworthy on them and thus higher likelihood of consumer forgiveness. Lastly, Guan and Sun (2023) find that high empathic response from chatbots increased consumer forgiveness intention during service remediation. 

To conclude, compared with service failures, the consumer forgiveness journey following brand transgressions and product‐harm crises remains significantly underexplored compared to the extensive research dedicated to forgiveness in the service failures context. This disparity in scholarly attention poses a crucial gap in understanding the complexities of consumer responses to brand transgressions and product‐harm crises, warranting a dire need for more comprehensive studies in these domains. Besides, in the service failures context, the involvement with nonhuman and autonomous agents have added another layer of complexity to the consumer forgiveness journey as they have been shown above to both influence the consumer attribution of the causes of wrongdoers and play a key role in facilitating forgiveness in the recovery stage. It warrants further research into the multifaceted impacts of nonhuman and autonomous agents in the consumer forgiveness in the service failures contexts and its possibility of extending to product‐harm crises and brand transgressions domains.

### 3.4.2 | Moderating mechanisms and related variables 

We conceptualize moderating variables as the key determinants that can intervene in the forgiveness process. This leads to the identification of 47 unique mediators from 39 quantitative studies in the literature. We organize these moderation effects into two themes, transgression tolerance and effective recovery, based on whether recovery was included in the research designs. Finally, we summarize the moderation variables by themes and characteristics in line with their impacts on consumer forgiveness and integrate our insights with the qualitative findings in the literature. 

##### 3.4.2.1 | Theme 1: Transgression tolerance 

In this theme, we include all the empirical studies that did not include an intervention of recovery in their research design. In these studies, researchers aim to investigate the factors that consumers attribute to negative marketing events as less severe and make them more forgiving. We identify 32 moderation variables from 23 studies. Among these variables, 17 are consumer‐related, 9 are relationship‐related, and 6 are brand‐related. 

The consumer‐related variables in this theme are tested under their potential influence on (1) increasing/decreasing the chance of perspective‐taking and (2) reducing the use of affective information in the thinking process. Concerning the likelihood of perspective‐ taking, empirical studies suggest a positive impact of secure attachment, holistic thinking style, and trait empathy on consumer forgiveness (Alnawas et al., 2023; Salagrama et al., 2021). Conversely, anxious attachment, analytical thinking, need for closure, and high construal levels are negatively associated with consumer forgiveness owing to the same rationale (Alnawas et al., 2023; Çelik et al., 2024; Hassey, 2019). Concerning reducing the use of affective information in the thinking process, researchers identify the positive effects of emotional intelligence, decision involvement, and cultural happiness on consumer forgiveness (Cheng, 2023; Papadopoulou et al., 2023; Tsarenko et al., 2019). These assumptions are aligned with the suggestions of coping theory and the interpersonal forgiveness literature (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; McCullough et al., 1997). However, it is noteworthy to mention that we have not witnessed any direct application regarding the moderation role of how consumers' chronic coping styles despite the coping theory being the most applied theoretical framework in the literature. 

The brand‐related variables associated with this theme are primarily examined under the theoretical accounts of social identity and in‐group favoritism (Tajfel & Turner, 1979). Empirical studies suggest consumers can be more forgiving of the transgressions made by home brands (Ge et al., 2023; Zourrig et al., 2015), peer‐based service providers (Rathee et al., 2022), and underdog brands (Septianto et al., 2020), compared to their counterparts. However, evidence also suggests that these positive characteristics could also lead to unforgiveness, referred to as the “origin‐backfire” effect, which occurs when consumers perceive threats and betrayals from transgressions committed by home brands (Davvetas et al., 2024). Some of the plausible underlying mechanisms explaining these contrasting findings are identified in the literature. Specifically, consumers can exhibit a lower tolerance for transgressions from home brands if they are idiocentric (Zourrig et al., 2015) or if they are from a loose culture (Ge et al., 2023). In the category of relationship‐related variables, we find that most of the relationship‐based measures, such as brand attachment (Flacandji et al., 2023), brand commitment (Lin & Chou, 2022), brand interaction frequency (Kuchmaner et al., 2024), parasocial relationship (Aw et al., 2022), relationship length (Chen et al., 2022c; Lin & Chou, 2022) play a positive role in consumer forgiveness. However, Wolter et al. (2019) find an exception: this buffering effect can backfire when consumers develop a sense of entitlement from their relationship with the brand.

##### 3.4.2.2 | Theme 2: Effective recovery 

There are 15 studies that compared the effectiveness of different transgression recovery strategies (e.g., apology vs. compensation), while exploring their boundary conditions. We find three core research streams: apology versus compensation (Aw et al., 2022; Casidy & Shin, 2015; Najam, 2022; Shin et al., 2018; Shuqair et al., 2021; Wei et al., 2020), the use of justification (Chen et al., 2022b; Tsarenko & Tojib, 2015), and other recovery fit (Atav et al., 2021; Ran et al., 2016; Wei & Ran, 2019) based on the nature of the research investigations. 

Apology versus compensation. Empirical evidence suggests that the nature of the transgression and consumer traits determine the decision to use an apology or compensation in service recovery. Studies suggest the use of apology is more contingent on the 

contexts, such as when the transgression is indirect to consumers (Casidy & Shin, 2015; Shin et al., 2018), when consumers perceive a communal relationship with the brand (Shuqair et al., 2021), when consumers have interdependent (vs. independent) self‐ construal (Aw et al., 2022), when consumers have an agreeable (vs. conscientious) personality (Najam, 2022), and when consumers perceive high power against the brand (Wei et al., 2020). In contrast, compensation is found to be an effective tool for achieving consumer forgiveness across most contexts, except for cases involving indirect transgressions and high‐power consumers (Casidy & Shin, 2015; Shin et al., 2018; Wei et al., 2020).

Two qualitative studies provide valuable insights into the moderating roles of consumer‐brand relationships (Finsterwalder et al., 2017) and consumer emotional intelligence (Mednini & Damak Turki, 2024) in the context of consumer forgiveness, complementing existing quantitative investigations. Their research demonstrates that consumers tend to engage with online brand communities when confronted with negative marketing events, utilizing these platforms to gather essential information for their attribution processes and to express their negative emotions. Consequently, the collective opinions and sentiments shared within these communities play a pivotal role in shaping consumers' forgiveness journeys as subsequent consumers may adopt prevailing narratives to assess the severity of the incident and the brand's responsibility. ### 4 | RESULTS 

### 4.1 | The proposed consumer forgiveness as a coping process 

Our aim in conducting this detailed literature review is to build a comprehensive conceptual framework to explain the consumer forgiveness process. The synthesis outlined in the previous sections have informed our theory‐driven integrative framework delineating salient psychological mechanisms of the consumer forgiveness process. In this section we introduce and explain this proposed framework. This framework also responds to the call for research by Kim et al. (2023) for an integrated theoretical framework to aid marketing researchers in explaining and understanding consumer forgiveness. We use the coping literature as the backbone of our framework and argue that forgiveness is a coping mechanism that consumers take to free themselves from the negative impacts of a negative marketing event. From this perspective, the overarching goal of forgiveness is to remove the present negative emotional arousal and to control future similar harms, losses, or threats to consumer self‐concepts (Lazarus & Folkman, 1984). The length of consumers' coping processes and the coping approach consumers use (e.g., forgiveness, avoidance) are subjective to the extent that the core objectives are achieved, at least under consumers' coping abilities (Epstein & Katz, 1992). Additionally, we integrate the theoretical arguments from the attribution, coping, justice, and trust theories to capture the cognitive activities within the two appraisal stages in the consumer forgiveness process (see Figure 3). Core mediation and moderation variables in our proposed consumer forgiveness process are consolidated with the empirical studies in the literature. 

We argue that the consumer forgiveness process entails cognitive, emotional, and motivational components. At the first step, the consumer forgiveness process begins when a stressor (or transgressor) induces distress for the consumer and damages the consumer‐ stressor relationship. At the primary appraisal stage, we suggest that consumers engage in harm evaluation and harm attribution. As attribution theory indicates, upon facing a stressor, the consumer appraises the severity, direction, intent, stability, and controllability of the harm inflicted. If the harm is deemed insufficiently impactful, the consumer may not exhibit negative reactions, rendering forgiveness unnecessary. Intent, stability, and controllability are indicative of predicting how consumers attribute the responsibility of the transgression to the transgressor. The lower the perceived responsibility, the higher the likelihood that consumers would forgive the transgression. Additionally, for the forgiveness process to initiate, consumers need to have the motivation to forgive, otherwise they will not pay attention to the recovery efforts by the transgressor. 

In the secondary appraisal stage, a concerted effort by the transgressing brand to facilitate recovery is imperative. Several different attempts by the stressor to recuperate a harmed relationship have been studied by marketing scholars. These recovery efforts include apologizing for the negative marketing event, compensating the consumer for the harm done, taking actions to prevent future

Guided by justice theory, we introduce the constructs of interactional, distributional, and procedural justice at this stage. Consumers evaluate recovery effectiveness with the extent to which they agree that interactional, distributive and/or procedural justice have been restored. Perceived justice predicts the likelihood that consumers can disassociate themselves from the stressors, namely the removal of the negative emotional state, the controllability of future similar harm, and the removal of threats to consumers' self‐ images (Blodgett et al., 1997; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Skarlicki & Folger, 1997). Consumers are likely to deploy forgiveness as their coping strategy when they perceive the source of the harm cease to exist. This may mean the perishment of the stressor or no likelihood of the transgression to reoccur. Consumers then will let go of the negative emotions, cognition and behavior and keep their patronage and connections with the brand. 

In sum, we propose that consumer forgiveness is an emotional, cognitive, and behavioral journey where consumers (1) acknowledge harmful behavior by stressor, (2) assess and perceive stressor as harmful, (3) acknowledge recovery efforts by stressor, (4) assess and perceive recovery efforts as effective, and (5) at the bare minimum let go of negative emotions, cognitions and behavior that is stimulated by stressor. 

Lastly, it is important to underline that consumer forgiveness and unforgiveness are distinct outcomes of the same process. Consumer forgiveness occurs when customers are satisfied with a company's recovery and trust repair efforts during the secondary appraisal stage. If these efforts are deemed unsatisfactory, forgiveness will not occur. When forgiveness is not achieved, retaliation may result, as 

consumers perceive the firm's actions as harmful and wrong, attributing blame to the company (Aquino et al., 2001; Beugré, 2005; Funches et al., 2009). Consumers who view recovery efforts as unsuccessful may choose retaliation as their coping strategy. This can involve behaviors such as brand switching, boycotting, or spreading negative word‐of‐mouth (Duhachek & Oakley, 2007; Worthington & Scherer, 2004). Retaliation, triggered by cognitive thoughts of harm, involves actions taken in response to an offense, often fueled by rage and anger (Allred, 1999). Revenge, a form of retaliation, involves aggressive behavior (Spencer, 1892). 

Another type of consumer unforgiveness is brand avoidance, where consumers opt not to engage in aggressive retaliatory actions but instead disengage from the brand. Brand avoidance occurs when consumers deliberately distance themselves from a brand to avoid negative experiences or associations (Lee et al., 2009). Similar to retaliation, avoidance also requires that customers are unsatisfied with a company's recovery and trust repair efforts during the secondary appraisal stage. Unlike retaliation, which involves overt negative behaviors, brand avoidance is a passive coping strategy where the consumer simply chooses not to engage with the offending brand. Brand avoidance highlights a less confrontational but equally damaging form of consumer unforgiveness, as it leads to long‐term disengagement and loss of customer loyalty. 

To the best of our knowledge, our consumer forgiveness process is the first holistic theoretical framework that integrates all conceptual, qualitative, and quantitative work in literature. Our framework consolidates the fragmented literature and provides a roadmap for future researchers about the core cognitive, emotional, and motivational components of the consumer forgiveness process.

### 5 | FUTURE RESEARCH AGENDA 

After a comprehensive synthesis of the literature, we identify six key avenues for future research, encompassing a total of 14 emerging research questions. These research agendas highlight critical gaps in the existing body of knowledge across three major areas: service recovery (incorporating justification), information processing (the roles of self‐concept maintenance, coping styles and emotions), as well as the contexts of AI and social climates. 

### 5.1 | Avenue 1: Further investigation of when and how to incorporate justification into recovery efforts to achieve consumer forgiveness 

The first research avenue focuses on the secondary appraisal stage of the consumer forgiveness process, specifically examining how brands can effectively incorporate justification (e.g., an apologia) for consumer forgiveness. Embedding justification is crucial, as it can help mitigate consumers' negative emotions and reduce potential legal or financial liabilities. (Coombs et al., 2010; You et al., 2020). Two pioneering studies demonstrate that this approach can effectively foster consumer forgiveness when dealing with uncontrollable failures and addressing consumers with a fresh‐start mindset (Chen et al., 2022b; Tsarenko & Tojib, 2015). Given that this research area is still in its early stages, we recommend future researchers investigate the question “What other contexts also support the incorporation of justification into recovery efforts to achieve consumer forgiveness?” 

Additionally, the optimal methods for incorporating justification remain largely underexplored in the consumer forgiveness literature. Theoretical frameworks from the field of communication may offer foundational insights for further scholarly inquiry. For instance, future research could examine the effectiveness of various justification tactics, such as differentiation and transcendence, in fostering consumer forgiveness (Benoit, 1995). Therefore, we also recommend that future scholars investigate the question, “What strategies can marketers implement to effectively incorporate justification into recovery efforts to achieve consumer forgiveness?.” 

### 5.2 | Avenue 2: Further investigation of the role of self‐concept maintenance in consumer forgiveness 

The second research avenue also centers on the secondary appraisal stage of the consumer forgiveness process with respect to the role of self‐concept maintenance behind consumer forgiveness. While existing empirical studies have primarily examined the impacts of justice and trust recovery—key resources that help consumers cope with negative marketing experiences—the role of self‐concept maintenance remains largely overlooked. This aspect is a critical determinant of forgiveness in the interpersonal forgiveness literature (Worthington et al., 2007). 

Two pioneering studies in consumer forgiveness provide a foundational basis for this exploration. Finsterwalder et al. (2017) 

conducted a qualitative investigation that classified the roles of self‐ interest and self‐protection in consumer forgiveness. Meanwhile, Tsarenko et al. (2019) highlighted further self‐concept maintenance factors, such as self‐healing and graceful acts. 

We propose that advancing this line of inquiry could help clarify a longstanding question for marketers: why do some consumers choose to forgive while others remain unforgiving, even when they receive similar levels of justice and trust recovery? We recommend future researchers consider questions such as “What makes forgiveness a good coping strategy for consumers?” and “What further self‐ concept maintenance‐based benefits do consumers expect when choosing to forgive a brand?” 

### 5.3 | Avenue 3: Further investigation of the impact of consumers' coping styles on consumer forgiveness 

While coping theory is the most widely applied framework in the consumer forgiveness literature, we have yet to identify any empirical investigations examining the moderating role of consumers' chronic coping styles in this context. Chronic coping styles refer to individuals' general strategies for managing stressful situations, which can include approaches like self‐control and seeking social support (Folkman et al., 1986). These coping styles serve as default patterns that influence how consumers attribute the causes of stressors, evaluate the impact of those stressors on themselves, and determine potential solutions (Beverland et al., 2010). 

In light of this context, we recommend that future researchers examine the role of consumers' chronic coping styles across the two appraisal stages in the forgiveness process. Specifically, researchers may explore the following questions: “How do consumers' coping styles shape their two stages of appraisal processes?” “How do these styles regulate the emotional impact in the forgiveness process?” Additionally, “How do these styles influence consumers' motivations to forgive?” Investigating these aspects could yield valuable insights into the intricate relationship between coping mechanisms and consumer forgiveness. 

### 5.4 | Avenue 4: Further investigation of the role of AI in consumer forgiveness 

Our fourth research avenue explores the impact of AI on consumer forgiveness across the two appraisal stages. Existing research on consumer forgiveness in the context of AI interactions is still underdeveloped, with limited studies examining how consumers forgive or fail to forgive AI‐driven service failures. While consumer forgiveness has been widely explored in human‐to‐human and brand relationships, most AI‐related literature has focused on topics like trust, acceptance, and adoption of AI technologies (Gursoy et al., 2019; Lu, 2019), leaving a significant gap in understanding the forgiveness process after AI failures. A notable exception is Robinson et al. (2020), which addresses consumer reactions to AI

service failures, but does not delve deeply into the forgiveness process. This gap is critical given the increasing reliance on AI in customer service, retail, telemedicine, and other consumer‐facing industries. Studies like those from Huang and Rust (2018), Lu (2019) and Paluch and Wirtz (2020) reveal that AI alters consumer perceptions of service quality. While AI minimizes interpersonal complexities such as human error or bias, it also lacks the emotional reciprocity that often facilitates consumer forgiveness (Bitner et al., 2000; Noor et al., 2023). Therefore, particularly as the absence of human empathy and connection of AI may impact the likelihood of consumer forgiveness of AI service failures. Hence, understanding how consumers react to AI transgressions is crucial for maintaining strong consumer‐brand relationships. 

Future research should explore how factors unique to AI interactions—such as the perceived intentionality of AI errors, the absence of human accountability, and the lack of emotional reciprocity—influence consumer forgiveness. Additionally, there is a need to investigate how consumers' emotional, cognitive, and behavioral responses to AI failures differ from those caused by humans. Future research can address the questions “How do consumer emotions, cognition, and behavior differ across human‐caused versus AI‐ caused transgressions?” and “What type of brand recovery is more effective for AI transgressions?”. Addressing these issues could offer critical insights into the consumer forgiveness process in AI‐human interactions and help brands develop effective recovery strategies to foster consumer forgiveness and rebuild trust. As AI plays a larger role in consumer interactions, understanding these dynamics will be key to long‐term success in AI‐powered services. 

### 5.5 | Avenue 5: Further investigation of the impact of social climates on consumer forgiveness 

We also identify a significant gap in the research concerning the impact of social climates on consumer forgiveness across the two stages of appraisal. Apart from a qualitative study by Casey et al. (2024), there is a lack of literature exploring this critical topic. Given the widespread influence of social media, we contend that social climates are vital to understanding the consumer forgiveness journey. Specifically, when a firm or brand commits wrongdoing, it can trigger a wave of negative attention across social networking sites and online brand communities. This phenomenon, referred to as an online firestorm, presents considerable challenges for businesses due to its viral nature and the extensive reach and potential destructiveness it entails (Chan et al., 2024; Pfeffer et al., 2014). 

Consequently, consumers' appraisal processes and emotional responses during the forgiveness process are likely shaped by the social climates. Given the significance of this phenomenon, we recommend that future researchers explore the following questions: “How do social climates influence consumers' appraisal processes?” “In what ways do these climates amplify the emotional effects of negative experiences on the forgiveness process?” Additionally, how do they impact consumers' motivations to forgive?” Investigating these aspects 

could yield valuable insights into how social climate dynamics inform the consumer forgiveness process. 

### 5.6 | Avenue 6: Further investigation of consumers' emotions in the forgiveness process 

Our final proposed research avenue focuses on the role of emotions in the forgiveness process. Existing literature has examined the relationships between five key emotions—anger, fear, empathy, sympathy, and compassion—and consumers' propensity to forgive (e.g., Atav et al., 2021; Ran et al., 2016). While these emotions reflect consumers' affective evaluations during the forgiveness process, they do not fully capture the spectrum of emotional experiences involved. For instance, negative marketing events may elicit moral emotions such as disgust and schadenfreude rather than harm‐related emotions like anger and fear (Haidt, 2003). Similarly, consumers may feel positive emotions like gratitude and thankfulness during effective service recovery rather than positive moral emotions such as empathy and compassion (Fredrickson, 2001). These understudied emotions may indicate new stressors or cognitive outcomes arising from the two stages of appraisal. Thus, we recommend that future research explore: “What additional understudied emotions are overlooked in the literature, and how might they shape the forgiveness process?” 

More importantly, based on the appraisal‐tendency framework, consumers' emotions also contain cognitive predispositions that can inform their thinking process and judgment (e.g., Lerner & Keltner, 2000). For instance, emotions such as anger and anxiety, which may arise from distinct transgressions, share a negative valence yet denote divergent motivational orientations—approach versus avoidance (Labroo & Rucker, 2010). In the realm of consumer forgiveness, the diversity of emotional experiences can lead to the adoption of various coping strategies, the allocation of cognitive resources, and the prioritization of different elements in the appraisal process. In light of this research gap, we propose that future investigations examine: “How do consumers' emotions systematically influence their cognitive appraisal processes?.” Table 6 summarizes our proposed future research avenues and emerging research questions.

### 6.2 | Implications for practice 

Our study contributes to theory by extending the current knowledge of consumer forgiveness processes. We add to the marketing literature by providing an extensive synthesis of how involved agents influence the consumer forgiveness process. We offer a more detailed, complete, and comprehensive understanding of different stages of the consumer forgiveness process by proposing an integrative theoretical framework. Furthermore, we intend that our framework can serve as a useful guiding tool for future marketing theorists and marketers in their analysis of consumer forgiveness behavior. We welcome future consumer forgiveness studies to map their theoretical grounding onto the framework we introduce in this 

Our study also provides implications for practitioners regarding achieving consumer forgiveness. First, we recommend marketing managers consider how consumers attribute the responsibility of the brand and how they appraise the transgression (e.g., loss or threat) before they decide on a crisis communication and recovery. These are the core tasks consumers undertake at the primary appraisal stage. The results will inform consumers' judgment of the transgression severity and determine the type of emotion elicited, such as anger or fear. Marketers need to consider all aspects above and ensure that their emotional and cognitive tones (e.g., Atav et al., 2021; Ran et al., 2016), their expectation of recovery strategies

(e.g., Casidy & Shin, 2015; Shin et al., 2018) meet consumers' expectations. Additionally, embedding some levels of justification or defense in the communication is possible ‐ only when consumers perceive the transgression as mild and when they don't have any fixed expectations about the brand in the first place (Chen et al., 2022b; Tsarenko & Tojib, 2015). 

As key to effective recovery, we suggest that the delivery of fairness and trustworthiness are crucial. Based on our research, these are the core elements consumers need to come to justice and conclude the consumer forgiveness process. When these conditions are met, consumers' motivations for forgiveness are higher. The perception of fairness can be delivered by ensuring consumers are treated respectfully, encouraging them to express their voices, and offering them fair recovery packages (Muhammad & Gul‐E‐ Rana, 2020; Muhammad & Mokhtar, 2017). Consumers' trust can be repaired when brands demonstrate their ability to deliver their recovery promises, show their genuine concern about consumers' welfare, and act morally (Xie & Peng, 2009). 

### 6.3 | Limitations and future research 

We acknowledge the following limitations in our study. Firstly, we synthesize the literature published in academic journals from the core databases and overlook the sources from conference proceedings. Future work may consider further types of sources while researching this agenda. Secondly, we adopted a thematic approach to synthesize the literature. Future researchers may consider using other approaches, such as meta‐analysis, to assess the robustness of findings in the consumer forgiveness literature (Paul & Barari, 2022).
