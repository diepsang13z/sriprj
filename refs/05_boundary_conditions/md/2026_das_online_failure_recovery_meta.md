## **1. Introduction** 

Online services have grown rapidly as smartphones and social media expand access to digital channels (Azemi et al., 2019). That reach widens firms’ customer bases yet raises the likelihood of online service failures, defined as instances where service providers do not meet customer expectations during online transactions (Haz´ee et al., 2017). Online service failure disrupt service delivery and carry serious consequences, including customer dissatisfaction, negative word-of-mouth, and customer defection, as switching service providers in an online 

context requires minimal effort (Fan et al., 2020). In this regard, a clear account of the mechanisms that cause online service failure and how recovery efforts shape subsequent responses is thus necessary. Yet, persistent inconsistencies in reported effects across online service failure call into question the reliability of recovery strategies to reduce negative reactions and produce favorable outcomes. 

Previous studies have investigated various facets of online service failure, including its antecedents (Odoom et al., 2020), consequences (Chen & Tussyadiah, 2021), and recovery strategies (Boshoff, 2007), wherein such studies often draw on attribution theory to explain how

Justice theory has also been used to explain how fairness in recovery (i.e., distributive, interactional, and procedural justice) affects customer outcomes (Lim et al., 2025). Yet, findings remain mixed, as some report significant effects for all justice dimensions on satisfaction (Migacz et al., 2018), whereas others find support only for selected dimensions (Nikbin et al., 2015). Similar tensions appear in attribution-based evidence, with some suggesting external attributions predominate (Mezulis et al., 2004) and others pointing to internal factors, such as customer self-efficacy, when explaining causes of failure (Dong et al., 2016). These contradictions, in turn, impede the development of clear guidance for online service failure recovery strategy. 

While meta-analytic studies on service failure and recovery exist for offline settings (e.g., Gelbrich & Roschk, 2011; Orsingher et al., 2010; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2014), online service failure differs in noteworthy ways. For instance, offline service failures often arise from employee behavior, facility breakdowns, inventory shortages, and queue delays, whereas online service failures commonly involve account lockouts, app crashes, checkout errors, cybersecurity breaches, payment failures, system latency, and website usability, among others. Evidence on outcomes diverges as well, wherein loyalty after recovery tends to be higher online (Shankar et al., 2023) while satisfaction and repurchase intention often show stronger effects in offline recovery (Harris et al., 2006). Directly transferring offline insights to online service failure and recovery can, therefore, generate misleading implications. 

Accordingly, this study proposes a comprehensive meta-analytic model of online service failure and recovery that integrates attribution theory and justice theory to synthesize fragmented findings and, in turn, provide consolidated estimates spanning the chain from the causes of online service failure to subsequent recovery (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Attribution theory (Weiner, 1985) clarifies who and what are held responsible for online service failure, whereas justice theory (Greenberg, 1990) clarifies how customers judge the fairness of recovery outcomes, procedures, and interactions, and thus their joint use traces the full pathway from failure genesis to recovery evaluation and the behaviors that follow. A meta-analytic approach is appropriate because it cumulates comparable effect sizes and tests moderators across contexts, yielding precise and generalizable estimates from diverse and often times conflicting studies (Lim, 2025). Hence, four questions guide the present study: _first_ , what do customers attribute to online service failure; _second_ , how do customers react to online service failure; _third_ , how do 

customers react to strategies that seek to recover from online service failure; and _fourth_ , what causes the variations in customer attributions and reactions to online service failure and recovery? 

The meta-analysis herein contributes to theory by advancing a unified model that connects two foundational but often siloed perspectives to comprehensively explain the process of online service failure, from causes of failure to recovery outcomes. The synthesis identifies sources of inconsistency across extant studies and clarifies where and when effects are likely to vary, closing a longstanding gap in online service failure research. The analysis also specifies how online service failure differs from its offline equivalent in attribution patterns, recovery expectations, and outcomes. Importantly, the results signal the need to treat online service failure as a distinct phenomenon that warrants strategies designed for the online environment with actionable guidance for tailoring recovery to contexts and segments. 

## **2. Literature review and hypotheses development** 

## _2.1. Attribution and outcome of online service failure_ 

Attribution theory (Weiner, 1985), when applied to online service failure, explains how customers assign causes for negative events, distinguishing whether the cause is placed with the customer, the service provider, or technology-related conditions. Noteworthily, prior work often considers service provider-related and technology-related causes as external attributions on the one hand and customer-related causes as internal attributes on the other (Luo & Chea, 2018). Once an online service failure occurs, attribution bias often shifts blame toward the service provider, which increases the likelihood of customer complaints (Bacile et al., 2020). The specific pathways are set out below. 

## _2.1.1. Customer-related factors and customer complaint_ 

Customer-related factors such as low participation in the process (Fan et al., 2020), insufficient knowledge of required steps (Wang & Zhang, 2018), and weak service compatibility (Zolfagharian et al., 2018) can precipitate online service failures. For example, low participation has been linked to higher failure incidence (Vakeel et al., 2018) while limited technology familiarity (Wang & Zhang, 2018) and poor compatibility (Li & Fang, 2016) disrupt service completion. Despite customer-driven origins, complaints frequently target the service provider because of attribution bias (Kocher ¨ & Paluch, 2019). ## _2.1.2. Service provider-related factors and customer complaint_ 

Service provider-related triggers include poor service quality (Lin et al., 2018), inaccurate or untimely information (Wang & Zhang, 2018), and weak service provider commitment (Luo & Chea, 2018). Customers often attribute online service failures to the service provider, which raises complaint likelihood (Nugroho & Wang, 2024). For instance, low electronic service quality has been shown to drive complaint behavior in electronic commerce (Rohden and Matos, 2022). Some studies note boundary conditions, whereby strong brand commitment can suppress complaints even when employees cause the failure (Weitzl & Hutzinger, 2019). Despite such contingencies, most findings indicate a positive association between service provider-related causes of service failure and customer complaints. As such: 

**H2.** Online service failures caused by service provider-related factors trigger customer complaints.

## _2.1.3. Technology-related factors and customer complaint_ 

Technology-related factors such as website design flaws (Kocher ¨ & 

Paluch, 2019), online payment problems (Gohary et al., 2016), and security concerns (Lin et al., 2018) are prominent causes of online service failure. Given that customers tend to externalize blame in online contexts, such causes are often assigned to the service provider, which increases customer complaint behavior (Fan et al., 2020). Notably, design complexity elevates complaint propensity (Wang & Zhang, 2018) and poor website experiences heighten frustration and complaints (Schiessl et al., 2023). A minority view suggests the bias is not universal, whereby in some self-service technology settings, customers may blame themselves and forgo complaining (Meuter et al., 2000). Even so, the majority of evidence favors a positive relationship between technology-related causes of service failure and customer complaints. As a result: 

**H3.** Online service failures caused by technology-related factors trigger customer complaints. 

## _2.2. Customer perception of justice_ 

Building on the earlier section, the attribution of causes for an online service failure often leads to complaints, a pattern consistent with attribution bias (Ross, 1977). Grounded in justice theory (Greenberg, 1990), the argument here is that once a complaint is lodged, the manner in which the service provider manages the complaint shapes perceptions of fairness. The literature on online service failure recovery converges on three justice dimensions in the handling of complaints: distributive justice, interactional justice, and procedural justice (Sengupta et al., 2018; Tahir, 2021). While distributive justice concerns the outcomes of recovery, including monetary and non-monetary compensation (McCollKennedy & Sparks, 2003), interactional justice concerns the quality of interpersonal treatment during recovery, including empathy, respectful communication, and sincere apology (Vazquez-Casielles et al., 2012´ ), whereas procedural justice concerns the fairness of the recovery process, including transparency, responsiveness, and adherence to clear policies (Ha & Jang, 2009). 

Evidence supports the relevance of each dimension, though not without debate. Tangible recovery outcomes anchor perceived distributive justice and often matter to customers who bear direct loss, with several studies emphasizing their centrality (Lim et al., 2025) while others argue that outcomes work best when paired with fair procedures and respectful interactions (Odoom et al., 2020). Empathetic communication and credible apologies tend to improve perceived interactional justice (Che et al., 2022), yet some studies caution that interpersonal treatment, on its own, may be insufficient to shift overall fairness judgments (Uruena ˜ & Hidalgo, 2016). Transparent and responsive procedures generally bolster perceived procedural justice (Che et al., 2022), though a few findings question its influence in certain settings (Wu & Huang, 2015). In this regard, the weight of evidence indicates that when complaint handling demonstrates fair outcomes, fair interpersonal treatment, and fair procedures, customers report higher postrecovery perceptions across all three justice dimensions. Consequently: 

**H4.** Handling customer complaints in ways that exhibit distributive, procedural, and interactional justice enhances post-recovery perceptions of (a) distributive, (b) procedural, and (c) interactional justice. 

## _2.3. Customer experience of justice_ 

While the previous section established how complaint handling shapes perceptions of distributive, procedural, and interactional justice, the next link in the present section concerns customer emotion. Perceived justice, understood as the alignment between what customers expect and what they receive in outcomes, interpersonal treatment, and processes, shapes emotional responses in online service failure (Kocher ¨ & Paluch, 2019). When received justice meets or exceeds expectations, customers report positive emotions such as relief and happiness, whereas when received justice falls short, customers report negative 

emotions such as anger, discomfort, disappointment, dissonance, and regret (Bahri Ammari et al., 2022). Findings, however, are not uniform across studies, as some report that distributive and interactional justice strengthen positive emotion while procedural justice does not (Kuo & Wu, 2012), whereas others find that procedural justice exerts the primary influence with limited effects from distributive and interactional justice (del Río-Lanza et al., 2009). Even with such variation, the weight of evidence supports a positive link from each justice dimension to emotion, which motivates the following hypotheses: 

**H5.** Customer experience of (a) distributive, (b) interactional, and (c) procedural justice have a positive impact on their emotion. 

## _2.4. Customer subsequent behavior_ 

Emotions that arise from justice evaluations are linked to key behavioral outcomes, including loyalty, switching, trust, and word-ofmouth (Azemi et al., 2019; Gohary et al., 2016; Manu et al., 2021; Ozuem et al., 2017). However, evidence on how emotions translate into these outcomes is not uniform, even when those emotions stem from distributive, procedural, and interactional justice. For instance, emotions, both positive and negative, have been shown to influence loyalty after evaluations of interactional and distributive justice, with limited effects observed after procedural justice (Chebat & Slusarczyk, 2005). Likewise, more recent evidence shows that positive emotions following distributive justice can stimulate favorable electronic word-of-mouth, whereas negative emotions do not always trigger unfavorable electronic word-of-mouth (Fu et al., 2015). Others show that when interactional and procedural justice are perceived as high in responses to online complaints, trust tends to rise through positive emotions, whereas the converse holds when negative emotions are salient (Olson & Ro, 2020). Similarly, lower perceived justice can intensify negative emotions and raise switching intentions while higher perceived justice can temper such intentions (Mody et al., 2020). Against this backdrop, the weight of evidence indicates that emotions formed in response to perceived recovery fairness shape downstream behavior. Given this: 

**H6.** Customer emotion after experiencing distributive, interactional, and procedural justice impacts their loyalty, switching, trust, and wordof-mouth. 

## _2.5. Boundary conditions_ 

The proposed meta-analytic model integrates attribution theory and justice theory. Even so, estimated relationships among model variables vary across extant evidence on online service failure. For example, perceptions of distributive and interactional justice have been found to shape emotions and intention to engage in word-of-mouth (Jung & Seock, 2017), whereas other work reports effects only for distributive justice, with limited or no effects for interactional or procedural justice (Choi & Choi, 2014). Meta-analysts commonly attribute such inconsistencies to differences in contextual and methodological features across studies (Haz´ee & Van Vaerenbergh, 2021). The present review identifies three _contextual_ , marketing-related factors—i.e., brand prestige (high versus low), offering type (product-based services versus true services versus pseudo services), and purchase frequency (high versus low)—and four _methodological_ , sample-related factors—i.e., age (young versus old), gender (male versus female), income (high versus low), and status of country development (developed versus developing)—as likely moderators.

**H8.** Offering type (product-based services versus true services versus pseudo services) moderates the relationships in the meta-analytic model. 

_Purchase frequency_<sup>2</sup> _(high_ versus _low)_ . Purchase frequency captures how often customers use an online service (Chang et al., 2012). Infrequent purchasers have been shown to spread more negative word-ofmouth after dissatisfying failures than frequent purchasers (Holloway et al., 2005). Justice components may also matter differently by frequency segment, for instance, interactional treatment tends to lift emotions and satisfaction among low-frequency customers, whereas procedural clarity and consistency tend to matter more for highfrequency customers (Chang et al., 2012). These patterns imply heterogeneous paths across frequency groups. On this basis: 

**H9.** Purchase frequency (high versus low) moderates the relationships in the meta-analytic model. 

## _2.5.2. Sample-related factors_ 

_Age (young_ versus _old)_ . Age differences are likely to shape paths in the meta-analytic model. In online gaming, distributive, procedural, and informational justice show overall positive effects on satisfaction and subsequent electronic word-of-mouth, yet younger gamers share more electronic word-of-mouth than older gamers, in part because peer guidance in gamer communities amplifies sharing among younger users (Ding & Lii, 2016). In group service failure settings, younger customers have been found to be more active in spreading negative word-of-mouth, consistent with stronger negative emotion and greater weight placed on perceived causes of failure (Albrecht et al., 2017). These patterns point to age-contingent differences in how justice evaluations translate into emotion and behavior. Accordingly: 

**H10** Age (young versus old) moderates the relationships in the metaanalytic model. 

_Gender (male_ versus _female)_ . Gender-based variation also appears in service failure research. Some studies report that women exhibit greater loyalty to service providers when recovery is handled well (Morgeson et al., 2020) while others find that men report higher loyalty under effective recovery (Azemi et al., 2019). In mobile shopping, men’s higher perceived ease of use of technology has been associated with stronger attribution of responsibility to providers for technology-related

_Income (high_ versus _low)._ Income can shift which justice components matter most. Among higher-income customers, distributive justice has been shown to exert the strongest effect on emotion and positive wordof-mouth after recovery, followed by procedural justice, whereas among lower-income customers, procedural justice takes precedence, followed by distributive justice (Keaveney & Parthasarathy, 2001). Such differences in blame attribution and expectation levels provide a clear basis for moderation. Thus: 

**H13** Status of country development (developed versus developing) moderates the relationships in the meta-analytic model. 

## **3. Methodology** 

## _3.1. Corpus curation_ 

## _3.1.1. Search_ 

An article search was conducted using the keyword “online service failure,” together with the alternatives “service failure and e-retailing” and “service failure and online booking,” across major databases and publisher platforms, including Academia.edu, EBSCO, Emerald, Gale, Google Scholar, J-Gate, JSTOR, ResearchGate, Sage, ScienceDirect, Science Open, Scopus, Springer, Taylor & Francis, and Web of Science. The same keywords were used to locate relevant theses on ProQuest and unpublished studies on the Social Science Research Network (SSRN). Consistent with prior meta-analyses (e.g., Mehta et al., 2022), a call for unpublished studies was issued via the Electronic List for Marketing Academics and Researchers (ELMAR). Reference lists of online service failure reviews were also screened to identify additional articles. This search process, in line with meta-analytic practice (Jebarajakirthy et al., 2024), yielded 301 articles. 

## _3.1.2. Filter_ 

Following the removal of 123 duplicates, the remaining 178 documents were retained if they (a) were written in English, (b) reported scholarly research, (c) examined online service failure—service failures occurring in online purchasing or online interactions with a firm or other stakeholders, (d) used quantitative methods, and (e) reported zero-order correlations or sufficient statistics for conversion (e.g., Student’s t). Applying the criteria led to the exclusion of 22 non-English articles, eight non-scholarly articles, three articles not focused on online service failure, and nine non-quantitative articles, resulting in 136 articles. Four articles that did not report the necessary (correlation) statistics were subsequently removed, resulting in 132 articles. At revision, 15 additional relevant articles were identified and added, bringing the final total to 147 (Fig. 2).

## _3.2. Meta-analysis_ 

_3.2.1. Assessment of publication bias_ 

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots (Maseeh et al., 2021), Rosenthal’s fail-safe _N_ (Rosenthal, 1979), and Egger’s regression (Mehta et al., 2022). In funnel plots, symmetry indicates a lower risk of publication bias (Hunter et al., 2014), whereas fail-safe _N_ was computed to estimate the number of missing studies that would be required to nullify the observed effects, wherein values exceeding five times the number of included studies ( _k_ ) plus 10 (5 _k_ + 10) suggest limited influence of publication bias (Lipsey & Wilson, 2001) while a non-significant intercept for Egger’s regression indicates no systematic small-study effects (Mehta et al., 2022).

## _3.2.2. Bivariate analysis_ 

Statistical power for each bivariate relationship was examined to gauge the likelihood of Type II error, that is, failing to reject a false null hypothesis (Mehta et al., 2022). Analyses used zero-order correlations. When zero-order correlations were unavailable, other statistics (e.g., _F_ ratios) were converted to correlations (Ashaduzzaman et al., 2022). Mean observed correlations and reliability-corrected correlations were then estimated. A random-effects model corrected correlation coefficients for measurement and sampling error by dividing each raw correlation by the square root of the associated Cronbach’s α (Chang & Taylor, 2016). After reliability correction, correlations were transformed to Fisher’s _z_ (Mehta et al., 2022), weighted by the inverse of their variances, and converted back to correlations for interpretation (Ashaduzzaman et al., 2022). Confidence intervals at the 95 % level were used to judge significance, whereby estimates whose intervals excluded zero were treated as significant at _p <_ 0.05 (Borenstein et al., 2011). 

## _3.2.3. Meta-analytic structural equation modeling (MASEM)_ 

Meta-analytic structural equation modeling (MASEM) was conducted in SPSS AMOS _v_ .27 to test direct paths. A pooled, meta-analytic correlation matrix served as the input. Variable means were set to zero and standard deviations to one. 

## _3.2.4. Assessment of heterogeneity_ 

Heterogeneity across studies was examined using the _Q_ statistic, the 75 % rule of thumb, and 80 % credibility intervals (Mehta et al., 2022). A significant Q suggests that sampling error alone cannot account for observed variance, implying the presence of moderators. Likewise, under the 75 % rule, if less than 75 % of observed variance is attributable to sampling error, effect sizes are considered heterogeneous and potential moderators are implicated. Similarly, wide 80 % credibility intervals point to meaningful between-study variation consistent with moderator influence. 

## _3.2.5. Moderation analysis_ 

Meta-regression was used to estimate the effects of candidate moderators on relationships within the model (Zablah et al., 2012). Several primary studies reported multiple effect sizes, which create dependence among estimates (Maseeh et al., 2021). ## _5.1. On online service failure and recovery_ 

## _5.1.1. From causes of failure to customer complaint_ 

Online service failures attributed to customer-related, service provider-related, and technology-related factors each increase customer complaint, with the strongest link observed for customer-related causes, followed by technology-related and service provider-related causes. This pattern implies that when digital interactions falter, customers tend to complain even when precipitating conditions lie partly with users or the surrounding technology, a tendency consistent with attribution bias in high-expectation environments where seamlessness is assumed as the default (Lovelock & Wirtz, 2016). 

## _5.1.2. From customer complaint to justice perceptions_ 

Customer complaint is positively associated with distributive justice, interactional justice, and procedural justice when the complaint is channeled into a recovery episode and handled with some effectiveness. The estimates reflect the net effect of the service provider’s response rather than a mechanical consequence of complaining. Post-complaint customers expect appropriate and tangible redress (McColl-Kennedy & Sparks, 2003), empathetic and respectful interpersonal treatment (Vazquez-Casielles et al., 2012´ ), and clear and transparent procedures (Ha & Jang, 2009). Interactional justice shows the largest association, followed by distributive justice and then procedural justice. This pattern 

aligns with technology-mediated contact where artificial intelligence and automation can raise efficiency but cannot replace acknowledgment and empathy, which restore a sense of being heard even when technology executes parts of the process (Park & Whang, 2022; Xu et al., 2019). This interpretation, however, does not claim that complaint guarantees fairness, rather, across observed recovery encounters, higher-quality responses are linked to higher justice perceptions, with interpersonal treatment carrying the greatest weight. 

## _5.1.3. From justice perceptions to emotions and outcomes_ 

Perceived justice shapes customer emotion, with interactional justice exerting the strongest influence, followed by distributive justice, and then procedural justice, which is positive yet smaller. These emotions, in turn, raise loyalty, trust, and word-of-mouth, and reduce switching, with the positive path to loyalty being the largest among the three favorable outcomes. This consolidated pattern clarifies mixed findings by showing that in digital contexts, where face-to-face cues are limited, respectful interpersonal treatment and fair outcomes carry greater weight than procedural fairness in restoring emotions and behavior (Crisafulli & Singh, 2017). 

## _5.1.4. Comparison with offline evidence_ 

Findings for online settings diverge from prominent offline metaanalyses that elevate distributive justice and place less emphasis on interactional justice (Orsingher et al., 2010; Ozkan-Tektas & Basgoze, 2017; Roschk & Gelbrich, 2014; Valentini et al., 2020; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2014; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2018). 

A prominent difference concerns the hierarchy of justice signals. In online recovery, interactional justice is the most influential driver of emotion, distributive justice follows, and procedural justice contributes

Another noteworthy difference concerns attribution and controllability. Offline syntheses often link higher complaint rates to failures that sit within service provider control and then tie those complaints to compensation as the most effective lever for satisfaction restoration (Orsingher et al., 2010; Roschk & Gelbrich, 2014; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2014). The online pattern shifts toward stronger complaint tendencies even when causes appear less controllable by the service provider, as seen in the relatively larger path from customer-related causes and a comparably strong path from technology-related causes to customer complaint, which is consistent with expectations of seamless digital performance and a tendency to hold the service provider accountable for coordination across interfaces and platforms. In this environment, interactional justice addresses the immediate relational breach by restoring a sense of being heard, with distributive justice reinforcing repair where tangible loss is salient, and procedural justice providing necessary, though less decisive, reassurance. 

## _5.2. On variations in online service failure and recovery_ 

Marketing-related (contextual) factors condition effects across the model. Brand prestige changes both expectations and reactions. Complaint propensity after technology-related failure is higher for highimage or prestige brands because customers anchor on elevated standards experience sharper disconfirmation when glitches occur (Maxham and Netemeyer, 2003; Van Doorn & Verhoef, 2008). Offering type also shifts pathways. Evidence has alternated between stronger effects of interactional justice on emotions for product-based services and stronger effects for true services; the consolidated pattern here favors product-based services, where tangible losses and clear redress expectations heighten the value of empathetic, respectful treatment after failure (Chen & Kim, 2019; Harris et al., 2006; Holloway et al., 2005). Purchase frequency further differentiates outcomes. High-frequency customers translate favorable post-recovery emotions into advocacy and retention more readily than low-frequency customers, a pattern that clarifies prior mixed results and points to the importance of managing emotional experience for frequent users (Arsenovic et al., 2023). 

Sample-related (methodological) factors similarly help explain several inconsistencies in the online service failure literature. Procedural justice tends to influence emotions more strongly among higher-income customers than among lower-income customers, consistent with evidence on income-linked expectations and entitlement clarity (Ding & Lii, 2016; Keaveney & Parthasarathy, 2001). Country development status also matters. Failures attributable to service provider-related causes prompt more complaints in developed markets, whereas technology-related failures prompt more complaints in developing markets, a pattern aligned with stronger external blame tendencies where institutional reliability is less certain (Rohden and Matos, 2022; Weber & Sparks, 2010). These differences suggest that customers in developed markets evaluate failures against higher baselines for process control and service quality while customers in developing markets display greater external attribution when technology falters (Malhotra et al., 2005; Yan et al., 2021). In practical terms, frontline responses in developed markets should foreground interactional justice through empathetic, culturally sensitive communication while recovery in developing markets should emphasize technology reliability and rapid troubleshooting (Chelminski & Coulter, 2011; Yan et al., 2021). 

Combined effects across marketing-related and sample-related moderators also emerge. Emotional responses to fair recovery drive loyalty and word-of-mouth more strongly among higher-income customers interacting with high-prestige brands, reflecting the weight such customers place on symbolic brand attributes and credible performance 

under stress (Das et al., 2024; Lim et al., 2025). Strategy therefore benefits from segmenting by income and calibrating recovery to prestige positioning, whereby prestige brands should match elevated expectations with visible interactional care and appropriate redress while nonprestige brands gain by signaling procedural clarity and dependable outcomes, particularly when serving lower-income segments or markets with weaker institutional reliability (Chelminski & Coulter, 2011; Malhotra et al., 2005; Yan et al., 2021). 

## **6. Conclusion** 

This meta-analysis establishes a coherent failure-to-recovery chain for online services, whereby customer-related, service provider-related, and technology-related causes increase customer complaint (strongest for customer-related), effective handling of such complaint elevates perceptions of distributive, interactional, and procedural justice (interactional most responsive), those justice perceptions lift customer emotion (again led by interactional), and such emotion raises loyalty, trust, and word-of-mouth while lowering switching. Moderator patterns (i.e., brand prestige, offering type, and purchase frequency on the marketing side and age, gender, income, and status of country development on the sample side) account for cross-study discrepancies and signal where recovery should be segmented and tailored. The online pattern departs from offline evidence by placing interactional justice, rather than distributive justice, at the center of emotional repair, which reframes both theory and practice for digital settings. The sections that follow detail the theoretical contributions, managerial implications, and directions for future research. 

## _6.1. Theoretical contributions_ 

This study makes four contributions to service failure research in general and to online service failure in particular. 

First and foremost, the meta-analysis herein treats online service failure as a full process (i.e., causes of online service failure to customer complaint to justice perceptions to customer emotion to customer subsequent behavior) within an integrated lens that combines attribution theory and justice theory. Attribution theory (Weiner, 1985) explains why customers frequently hold service providers responsible for failures arising from customer-related, service provider-related, and technologyrelated causes, a bias that manifests as customer complaint. Justice theory (Greenberg, 1990) then explains how post-complaint expectations of distributive, interactional and procedural justice shape customer emotion and downstream behavior. Alignment between expected and received justice tends to elicit positive emotions and favorable outcomes, whereas misalignment tends to generate negative emotions and switching intentions. A large evidence base (147 studies, 82,901 individuals, 24 countries) yields convergent and precise support for these links and clarifies where effect sizes are strongest within the chain. 

Second, the moderation findings extend theory by showing when and for whom complaining tendencies, justice perceptions, and emotions are most affected. Country development status and income shift key paths in ways consistent with established frameworks. In more individualistic, higher-expectation contexts that characterize many developed markets, accountability concerns heighten complaint tendencies when failures are attributed to service providers (Hofstede, 1980; Weber & Sparks, 2010). Where digital infrastructure is thinner and technology hurdles are more salient, technology-related failures more readily trigger complaints and negative emotions, consistent with the unified theory of acceptance and use of technology’s (UTAUT) emphasis on effort expectancy and facilitating conditions (Malhotra et al., 2005; Venkatesh et al., 2003; Yan et al., 2021). Offering type and purchase frequency also matter, as interactional justice has a stronger impact on emotions in product-based settings where tangible loss and clear redress expectations heighten the value of empathetic and respectful treatment (Chen & Kim, 2019; Harris et al., 2006; Holloway et al., 2005) while emotions

These patterns open avenues to connect online service failure with cultural dimensions theory (Hofstede, 1980) and with affective events theory (Weiss & Cropanzano, 1996), which predicts stronger emotional responses when customers appraise service encounters as personally significant. 

Third, the meta-analytic evidence herein elevates interactional justice as the pivotal driver of customer emotion in online recovery, exceeding the influence of distributive and procedural justice. Customer complaint shows the strongest association with interactional justice and this form of justice shows the strongest association with customer emotion, which then shape their loyalty, switching, trust, and word-ofmouth. This emphasis, in turn, implies boundary conditions for automated recovery. Noteworthily, automated agents built on artificial intelligence improve consistency and increase speed yet often lack empathic signals that support emotional repair and interactional justice, and thus, human agents can supply those signals while well-designed anthropomorphic cues can narrow the gap (Pavone et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2025). Hybrid recovery models (e.g., automated triage plus human engagement during moments that matter) also fit the pattern of effects observed and translate the theoretical primacy of interactional justice into design guidance. 

Last but not least, the online context differs meaningfully from offline context of service failure, thus warranting separate theorizing and evidence synthesis. Notably, influential offline meta-analyses tend to place distributive justice at the center of recovery satisfaction and to assign smaller roles to interactional justice (Orsingher et al., 2010; Roschk & Gelbrich, 2014; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2018; Valentini et al., 2020). The present findings reverse that emphasis for online settings, with interactional justice exerting the strongest effect on customer emotion and distributive justice next, and thus, they uniquely document how customer-related, service provider-related, and technology-related causes differentially heighten customer complaint—an attribution layer not unpacked by prior syntheses (Ozkan-Tektas & Basgoze, 2017; Van Vaerenbergh et al., 2014). Characterizing online service failure as a distinct phenomenon in this way, therefore, advances theory on service failure and recovery in digital environments and sets a clearer agenda for future research. 

## _6.2. Managerial implications_ 

Online service failures continue to disrupt service delivery despite advances in digital channels, which requires deliberate recovery design. The evidence points to several actionable priorities. 

First and foremost, interactional justice deserves primacy in online recovery, since empathetic, respectful communication most strongly shapes customer emotion. Automated agents can be equipped to recognize and respond to affect through natural language processing and emotion recognition, supported by service scripts that adapt content and tone to customer state—a direction that balances relational value with operational efficiency and is consistent with service-dominant logic (Vargo & Lusch, 2004, 2008) and social response theory (Nass & Moon, 2000; Reeves & Nass, 1996), which both emphasize responsive, relational exchange. 

Second, recovery should be individualized by linking customer profiles, failure causes, and expected justice dimensions within customer relationship management systems that surface guidance to agents in real time. During payment failures, for instance, prompt identification of affected customers and targeted responses that explain steps taken, timelines, and restitution levels can raise perceived procedural clarity and distributive fairness at the moment they matter most. 

Third, sensitivity to technology-related failures varies with brand prestige, gender composition, and country development status. In markets with weaker institutional reliability, customers tend to externalize blame when technology falters, which elevates complaints for 

technology-related incidents, and such instances, rapid troubleshooting and clear status updates should, therefore, be front-loaded, together with resilient back-end processes. Therefore, prestige brands should anticipate sharper reactions when expectations are violated and precommit to visible recovery standards for technology-related incidents while avoidance of stereotypes and gender-sensitive language should be embedded in both automated and human interactions to reduce friction in complaint handling. 

Fourth, exposure to service provider-related failures is not uniform across brands and offerings. Tighter margins for error arise for highprestige brands on technology-related failures and for product-based services where interactional treatment most strongly shapes customer emotion, and thus, high-prestige brands should pre-commit to rapid diagnostics, transparent status updates, and proportionate restitution for technology-related incidents, whereas providers of product-based services should foreground empathetic, respectful interpersonal communication and close the loop with simple procedures and fair outcomes while targeted loyalty benefits can recognize customers who remain engaged through recovery. 

Fifth, complaints arising from customer-related failures are more likely when customers are less experienced or engage infrequently, which argues for clear pre-emptive guidance, process simplification, and timely micro-education at points of friction, thus error-prevention design patterns along with instructional nudges and walkthroughs lower the probability of failure attribution spiraling into complaints. 

Sixth, the translation from customer complaint to justice perceptions is more elastic under prestige conditions, with the strongest movement observed for interactional justice, therefore, frontline teams should be trained to communicate with empathy and precision while complaint systems should make procedures transparent and outcomes proportionate to loss, using clear timelines and status cues that customers can track without effort. 

Seventh, the impact of interactional justice on customer emotion is stronger for prestige brands and varies by offering type and market segment, therefore, personalization at the level of salutation, recall of prior interactions, and explicit acknowledgment of inconvenience, followed by post-resolution follow-ups, strengthens emotional repair and translates into loyalty, trust, and word-of-mouth, particularly for product-based services and in higher-expectation markets. 

Last but not least, across these actions, context and segment should guide emphasis, for instance, prestige positioning heightens expectations and penalties for failure, technology-related incidents in developing markets require robust diagnostics and clear communication, and customers who are inexperienced or infrequent benefit most from preventive design and just-in-time guidance, so that interactional care, procedural clarity, and fair outcomes are delivered in concert and at speed. 

## _6.3. Limitations and future research directions_ 

This study enriches the literature on online service failure while carrying several limitations. 

First and foremost, the quantitative scope of the meta-analysis excluded qualitative studies and the English-language restriction narrowed coverage to a subset of the global evidence base. Generalizability would improve by incorporating non-English scholarship and qualitative insights that unpack context, process, and meaning. 

Second, several moderation effects could not be tested because underlying primary studies did not report sufficient statistics. For instance, interactions of customer-related causes with brand prestige, income level, and status of country development status could not be examined. Harmonization choices during coding, despite high inter-coder agreement, may also leave residual category ambiguity when primary labels differ for similar constructs. 

Third, future research should extend the theoretical lens beyond attribution theory and justice theory. For instance, affect control,

The next step, in turn, is a meta-analytic model that nests these perspectives and tests where each is most predictive along the chain from causes to customer complaint to justice perceptions to emotions to outcomes. 

Fourth, temporal dynamics merit focused attention. Cross-sectional designs dominate the current evidence, yet perceptions after an online service failure evolve as customers receive updates, experience partial remedies, or encounter subsequent transactions. Longitudinal designs (e.g., event-study analyses around incident windows, field experiments that manipulate recovery levers over time, panel surveys) would clarify how justice signals cumulate or decay and when emotions convert into durable advocacy, loyalty, trust, or exit. 

Fifth, cultural context remains underexplored. Culture shapes expectations for accountability, compensation, and interpersonal treatment, as dimensions such as individualism, power distance, and uncertainty avoidance (Hofstede, 1980) likely condition the direction and strength of links in the model. The MASEM herein, however, could not include these moderators due to insufficient reporting in the primary literature. Hence, purpose-built primary studies should test them directly and report statistics that enable future syntheses. 

Last but not least, measurement and method improvements would strengthen inference. Greater use of behavioral outcomes (e.g., actual switching, repeat purchase, and observed word-of-mouth) alongside self-reports, standardized reporting of reliability and intercorrelations, and consistent operationalization of justice components would reduce unexplained heterogeneity. Mixed-method designs that pair qualitative tracing of recovery episodes with quantitative estimation can further reveal mechanism while preserving comparability, which, in turn, would enable finer-grained moderator tests and help map which recovery levers matter most for which customers and contexts.
