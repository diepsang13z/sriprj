### **1. Introduction** 

Imagine a couple celebrating their anniversary at a prestigious restaurant chosen for its awards and strong reputation. Upon arrival, they discover that their reservation is missing and no table is available. Trusting the venue’s reputation, they initially wait in silence, expecting the issue to be resolved. As time passes with vague reassurances and no visible progress, frustration grows. Eventually, the couple speaks up, explaining the importance of the occasion, sharing preferences, and suggesting alternatives. Their intervention reflects not an intent to collaborate, but a recognition that recovery requires their input to progress. 

Service failures are inevitable in hospitality contexts, where interactions are high-contact, emotionally charged, and often tied to milestone events such as celebrations or long-anticipated experiences (Bitner et al., 1990; Zeithaml et al., 1993). While service recovery research has traditionally focused on firm-led actions to restore satisfaction, it has paid comparatively less attention to customer-initiated participation during recovery as a mechanism shaping outcomes. Yet, 

customer participation during recovery is not uniform; customers do not always intervene to articulate their needs, offer suggestions, or propose solutions. 

These behaviors fall under the broad construct of customer participation (CP), which has typically been framed as proactive, firm-invited value co-creation (Bendapudi and Leone, 2003; Dong et al., 2008). This dominant framing, however, does not fully capture the situational nature of CP in failure settings. When customers intervene under conditions of severe disruption, their actions are often not motivated by engagement or shared responsibility, but by the recognition that progress depends on their involvement. Participation in these situations is prompted not by an invitation to co-create, but by the need to engage with a stalled recovery process. 

This research extends service recovery theory by explaining a decision point that dominant frameworks have largely overlooked: when customers choose to intervene during recovery, rather than how they evaluate outcomes after recovery has occurred. Our framework identifies the conditions under which customers are more or less likely to engage in CP during recovery. By focusing on participation as a situationally triggered behavioral response rather than an evaluative reaction or a firm-invited role, we offer a process-oriented account of customer action during service recovery. 

Drawing on control theory (Carver and Scheier, 1981; Hui and Bateson, 1991), we propose that CP arises when customers experience a disruption to the service encounter that creates a gap between their desired state and the unfolding experience, threatening their sense of control in pursuing their goals. This perceived loss of control heightens customers’ motivation to intervene. In this context, CP functions as a solution-oriented behavioral response through which customers seek to reassert control by articulating needs, offering suggestions, and actively engaging with employees to shape the resolution. Such action is aimed at influencing the recovery process and preventing further deterioration, rather than co-creating value. 

Importantly, reactive participation is not determined solely by the occurrence of failure itself. It is shaped by contextual cues that signal whether customer involvement is expected, necessary, or appropriate. We focus on two such cues: quality signals and employee responsiveness. Drawing on signaling theory (Spence, 1973), we argue that quality signals such as awards, certifications, or platform recognitions may attenuate the likelihood that failure severity translates into CP by shifting perceived responsibility for recovery toward the firm. In contrast, employee responsiveness shapes the extent to which participation translates into improved recovery outcomes once it occurs, by signaling acknowledgment and action on customer input. Across five studies, this research contributes to the service recovery and hospitality literatures by offering a process-oriented account of CP, clarifying when it emerges, the conditions under which it occurs, and how contextual cues shape both participation initiation and its effectiveness. 

### **2. Proposed framework and hypotheses development** 

### _2.1. Customer participation as a solution-oriented response in service recovery_ 

Customer participation (CP) in service recovery has traditionally been conceptualized as a co-creative process in which firms and customers jointly solve problems to enhance satisfaction and value (e.g., Dong et al., 2008; see Supplement file A for a review). This perspective emphasizes proactive, firm-invited involvement and assumes that customers participate when they are willing and prepared to do so. However, emerging research suggests that CP can also arise as a reactive response, driven not by a desire to co-create but by situational breakdowns that compel customers to intervene when service delivery falters (Joosten et al., 2017; Guo et al., 2016). 

Building on this insight, we define CP as a solution-oriented behavioral response through which customers contribute input aimed at influencing how recovery unfolds. This includes behaviors such as suggesting alternatives, articulating preferences, and engaging in problem-solving. This framing distinguishes CP from two adjacent constructs. First, unlike proactive, firm-facilitated co-creation (Vargo and Lusch, 2016; Chan et al., 2010), CP in recovery contexts is situationally triggered rather than role-invited. Second, unlike complaint or voice behaviors, which primarily involve communicating dissatisfaction or assigning blame (Bell and Luddington, 2006), CP reflects efforts directed toward influencing how the problem is addressed. This distinction is particularly salient in hospitality contexts, where service delivery unfolds in real time and customers often lack predefined pathways for redress (Kim and So, 2023). 

Control theory provides a useful lens for explaining when such participation becomes more likely. Service failures create a discrepancy 

between customers’ desired state and the unfolding experience, particularly when recovery progress appears uncertain or stalled (Carver and Scheier, 1981; Hui and Bateson, 1991). This discrepancy motivates corrective action aimed at restoring alignment with one’s goals. Importantly, such disruptions can give rise to multiple responses apart from CP, including withdrawal, exit, or complaint (Khamitov et al., 2020). In contrast, we focus on CP as a solution-oriented behavioral response through which customers attempt to engage with a disrupted recovery process. 

As failure severity increases, the perceived stakes of the situation become more salient, heightening customers’ motivation to intervene to prevent further loss or misalignment (Roggeveen et al., 2012). Under these conditions, customers may be more likely to engage in CP by articulating needs or offering suggestions. We therefore expect that greater failure severity increases the likelihood that customers engage in CP, while recognizing that this response remains contingent on contextual conditions examined in subsequent sections. 

**Hypothesis 1** . Service failure severity increases the likelihood that customers engage in customer participation during service recovery. 

### _2.2. Customer participation as a process mechanism in service recovery_ 

Service recovery research has demonstrated that customer participation (CP) can influence outcomes such as satisfaction and word-ofmouth when customers contribute to problem solving (e.g., Dong et al., 2008; Haz´ee et al., 2017). Prior work, however, has predominantly examined CP as a direct predictor of recovery outcomes, leaving its role within the recovery process itself less clearly specified. We extend this perspective by positioning CP as a solution-oriented behavioral response that operates as a process-relevant pathway linking service failure severity to recovery outcomes. This repositioning does not preclude CP from functioning as a direct predictor; rather, it specifies how failure severity translates into recovery outcomes through customer action. 

Drawing on control theory, we locate CP within a broader selfregulatory process rather than treat it as a standalone driver of positive outcomes. Service failures, particularly those of high severity, generate a discrepancy between customers’ desired and actual states, thereby activating a motivation to restore control (Carver and Scheier, 1981). This motivation arises from the discrepancy itself rather than from an expectation that any particular response will prove effective. Accordingly, CP is not premised on a belief that participation will necessarily succeed; rather, it reflects an attempt to engage with a disrupted recovery process under conditions of threatened control. 

Within this process, CP represents the action through which customers respond to the discrepancy by articulating preferences, offering suggestions, or engaging with employees regarding the resolution. These behaviors constitute a pathway through which customer input enters the unfolding recovery process. Importantly, this pathway does not offset the direct negative impact of service failure on customer evaluations, which remains pronounced. Instead, it captures how customer action becomes incorporated into the recovery process when participation occurs. 

Taken together, we propose that CP mediates the relationship between service failure severity and recovery outcomes by serving as a behavioral pathway through which customers attempt to influence the trajectory of recovery. 

**Hypothesis 2** . Customer participation (CP) mediates the effect of service failure severity on (a) satisfaction with the service encounter and (b) word-of-mouth. 

### _2.3. The role of quality signals_ 

The preceding section established that failure severity activates a discrepancy that motivates a behavioral response, and that CP

represents one pathway through which customers attempt to reduce this discrepancy. In this study, we focus on quality signals as a key contextual factor shaping how the relationship between service failure severity and CP unfolds. While the control-based framework explains why motivation to restore control arises, an important open question is how customers choose to restore that control. Specifically, contextual cues may shape whether customers intervene directly or rely on provider-driven resolution. Quality signals are examined as a critical boundary condition that may paradoxically discourage the participation that failure severity would otherwise elicit. 

Hospitality firms frequently signal service quality through awards, certifications, and ratings (Sutherland et al., 2021). Drawing on signaling theory (Spence, 1973; Kirmani and Rao, 2000), such cues convey provider competence and reliability, thereby reducing uncertainty when evaluating service quality directly is difficult (Nicolau and Sellers, 2010). By projecting reliability and capability, quality signals shape customers’ role expectations, including assumptions about how responsibility for managing failures should be allocated (Soberman, 2003). 

Control theory suggests that while individuals are motivated to reduce discrepancies between desired and actual states (Carver and Scheier, 1981), it does not prescribe how control must be restored; rather, individuals select among alternative strategies for regaining control. In this context, quality signals do not diminish discrepancy-driven motivation. Instead, they shape how control is pursued. Visible quality cues communicate that the provider possesses both the capability and authority to resolve disruptions independently, reducing information asymmetry and shifting perceived responsibility toward the firm (Spence, 1973; Kirmani and Rao, 2000). When failure occurs in the presence of strong quality signals, customers may infer that recovery falls within the firm’s domain of competence and rely more on provider-driven resolution rather than initiating direct intervention themselves (Hui and Toffoli, 2002). 

Thus, while quality signals reduce perceived risk (Chen et al., 2024; Helm and Mark, 2007), they simultaneously raise the threshold at which failure severity triggers direct participatory action. Under high failure severity, the motivation to regain control remains strong. However, quality signals shape how customers interpret responsibility, shifting their chosen response toward relying on the provider to manage recovery rather than initiating direct intervention themselves, particularly when the need to regain control is less acute. In this way, quality signals do not eliminate participation but condition when and how it is enacted. Accordingly, we hypothesize: 

**Hypothesis 3** . Quality signals moderate the effect of service failure severity on CP, such that the positive effect of failure severity on CP is weaker when quality signals are present. 

### _2.4. The amplifying role of employee responsiveness_ 

Employee responsiveness refers to the extent to which frontline employees acknowledge customer input and take visible action in response during service recovery. In high-contact hospitality contexts, responsiveness shapes how customers experience recovery interactions once participation has been initiated (Tung et al., 2017). Importantly, responsiveness is defined here as the acknowledgment and action taken in response to customers’ input, rather than as politeness, courtesy, or generalized interaction quality. 

When customers engage in CP, they attempt to influence the recovery process by articulating their needs, offering suggestions, or engaging with employees to resolve the issue. Whether these efforts translate into improved outcomes depends not only on customers’ actions but also on how the firm responds. Responsive employees signal that customer input has been heard and acted upon (Tung et al., 2017), reinforcing the perception that their participation translates into improved recovery outcomes. Within the control-theoretic framework, experienced 

responsiveness serves as a feedback mechanism through which customers assess whether their participation has reduced the discrepancy between the desired and actual service state. This feedback function enhances CP’s capacity to restore a sense of control during recovery. 

This conceptualization distinguishes responsiveness from related constructs such as interactional justice and double deviation (Bitner et al., 1990). Interactional justice emphasizes interpersonal treatment, respect, and dignity (e.g., Tax et al., 1998), whereas responsiveness captures whether customer input has an observable impact on the recovery process. Similarly, low responsiveness is not conceptualized as a second service failure or a failed recovery attempt, but rather as a feedback condition indicating whether participatory efforts are effectively integrated into recovery actions. When responsiveness is low, customers may perceive their participation as ineffective, weakening the positive effects of CP even if the substantive issue is ultimately resolved. 

In line with this reasoning, employee responsiveness is modeled as primarily salient at the post-participation stage. While anticipatory expectations about responsiveness may inform participation decisions, customers at the initiation stage operate under uncertainty about how the firm will respond, relying instead on broader contextual cues such as quality signals to guide their action selection. Responsiveness becomes more diagnostic and consequential after CP has been initiated, when customers can directly evaluate whether their input has been acknowledged and acted upon. This stage-salience reflects the temporal structure of live service recovery encounters: firms rarely have a ready-made solution at the moment a failure is identified; rather, employees must assess the situation and determine feasible responses through interaction with the customer. Responsiveness becomes most diagnostic as employees engage with customer input, although anticipatory expectations about responsiveness may also shape participation decisions. We therefore hypothesize: 

**Hypothesis 4** . Employee responsiveness moderates the effect of CP on (a) satisfaction and (b) word-of-mouth, such that the positive effects are stronger when responsiveness is high. 

### _2.5. Conflicting signals: when quality cues undermine the impact of responsiveness on CP_ 

Employee responsiveness can enhance the positive outcomes of CP by reinforcing the perceived impact and legitimacy of customer input. However, this effect may be limited by quality signals that shape expectations before recovery interactions begin. Together, these influences reflect a functional differentiation within the recovery process: quality signals operate as ex ante cues that shape how customers interpret the situation by influencing the perceived allocation of control between the customer and the firm, whereas employee responsiveness operates as ex post feedback, providing diagnostic information about whether enacted participation is acknowledged and meaningfully influences the recovery process. This distinction follows from control theory, which differentiates between cues that guide action selection and feedback that evaluates action effectiveness (Carver and Scheier, 1981). The differentiation is analytical rather than strictly temporal; both factors may be present throughout the encounter but exert their primary influence through distinct mechanisms. 

As established above, quality signals shape whether customers perceive direct intervention as necessary by influencing the actionselection stage of the control process. By contrast, employee responsiveness serves a diagnostic function once participation has been initiated. When customers provide input, responsive behavior can validate their efforts and translate participation into improved recovery outcomes, serving the feedback-evaluation function within the control process. However, this feedback function becomes consequential only when participation occurs. 

This asymmetry implies that quality signals primarily shape whether failure severity translates into participatory action, whereas

### **3. Overview of studies** 

To test the proposed framework, we employ a multi-method research design combining qualitative, field, and experimental approaches 

(Fig. 1). This design enables us to establish the core relationships, validate them across contexts, and examine the boundary conditions that shape when and how customer participation (CP) emerges and translates into recovery outcomes. 

Studies 1–3 focus on establishing and validating the core process linking service failure severity, CP, and recovery outcomes. Study 1 uses the Critical Incident Technique to examine whether failure severity motivates CP (H1) and whether CP functions as a behavioral pathway linking severity to satisfaction and word-of-mouth (H2). Study 2 extends this test using large-scale online review data, providing behavioral validation of CP as an enacted response in real-world service settings. Study 3 offers causal evidence through a controlled experiment, replicating the mediating role of CP under tightly controlled conditions. 

Building on this foundation, Study 4 introduces quality signals as a key boundary condition and examines their moderating role in shaping whether failure severity translates into CP (H3). This test establishes that participation is contingent not only on the level of disruption but also on contextual cues that shape perceived responsibility for recovery.

### **4. Study 1: Establishing the mediating effect of CP** 

To deepen our understanding of CP in service recovery, our initial step involved employing the Critical Incident Technique (CIT) to gather data. Study 1 primarily aimed to empirically examine the relationship between service failure severity and CP (Hypothesis 1). At the same time, we aimed to investigate the role of CP as an underlying mechanism (Hypothesis 2) in the relationship between service failure severity and two specific service outcomes: (a) satisfaction with the service encounter, and (b) word-of-mouth. 

### _4.1. Method_ 

We recruited 167 participants from a subject pool managed by the business school at one of the largest Australian public universities. Each participant was asked to recall and describe in detail a recent service failure they experienced. Additionally, they were prompted to describe the actions taken by both themselves and the service provider during and after the incident to capture CP. Only narratives that provided a detailed account of the service failure and subsequent actions were retained for analysis, resulting in a final sample of 158 participants (63% females; _Ma_ ge= 19.95, _SD_ age = 2.08). The narratives primarily featured service failures in retail outlets (28%), airline companies (18%), hotels (11%), and restaurants (9%). 

Three independent assessors evaluated and coded the customer narratives to rate the severity of CP and service failure on a scale of 1–7, averaging the scores to compute variable values (see Supplement file A for scale items). Inter-rater reliability was assessed using an intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) to evaluate the consistency of coders' ratings of the written statements. The resulting ICCs for service failure severity and CP, respectively ( _ICC_ Severity = 0.95; _ICCC_ P = 0.93), were acceptable (Cicchetti, 1994), indicating a high degree of agreement. This suggests that CP and service failure severity were rated similarly by the independent assessors ( _M_ Severity = 4.22, _SD_ Severity = 1.39; _M_ CP = 3.46, _SD_ CP = 1.64). 

Participants rated their level of overall satisfaction with service encounter using three items adapted from Gelbrich (2011) and Maxham III, 2001 ( _α_ = 0.98, _M_ = 2.92, _SD_ = 1.70) and word-of-mouth with 1 item from Maxham III, 2001 (‘I would recommend this service provider to other people’, _M_ = 3.46, _SD_ = 1.82). The relationship length with the service provider was used as a control variable and measured in months ( _M_ = 34.27, _SD_ = 50.6). 

### _4.2. Results_ 

Hayes’ PROCESS macro (Model 4) based on 10,000 bootstrap samples with a 95% bias-corrected interval was used to test the mediation effect of CP on the relationship between service failure severity and service outcomes (Hayes, 2022). Results show a significant positive effect of service failure severity on CP ( _b_ = 0.37; _t_ = 4.20, _p <_ 0.001), supporting Hypothesis 1. Results highlight a significant and positive effect of CP on (a) satisfaction with service encounter ( _b_ = 0.33, _t_ = 4.04, _p <_ 0.001) and (b) word-of-mouth ( _b_ = 0.29, _t_ = 3.43, _p <_ 0.001), while service failure severity has a significant negative effect on both service outcomes (satisfaction with service encounter: _b_ = − 0.48, _t_ = − 5.08, _p <_ 0.001; word-of-mouth _b_ = − 0.56, _t_ = − 5.71, _p <_ 0.001). Results also reveal a significant indirect effect of CP in the 

relationship between service failure severity and (a) satisfaction with service encounter _(b_ = 0.12, CI= [0.05; 0.21]), and (b) word-of-mouth ( _b_ = 0.11, CI = [0.04; 0.20]), supporting Hypothesis 2 for partial mediation as the direct effects of service failure severity on (a) satisfaction with the service encounter ( _b_ = − 0.48; _t_ = − 5.07, _p <_ 0.001) and (b) word-of-mouth ( _b_ = − 0.56; _t_ = − 5.71, _p <_ 0.001) are significant. 

### **5. Study 2: Validating the mediating role of CP through behavioral data (Online reviews)** 

Study 2 aims to strengthen the validity of Hypothesis 2 by examining the mediating role of CP in service recovery, particularly in the relationship between service failure severity and service outcomes (satisfaction with the service encounter and word-of-mouth). To achieve this, we analyze field data sourced from online customer reviews that vividly portray instances of service failure. In this study, we conceptualize online reviews as enacted electronic word-of-mouth (eWOM) rather than as stated intentions to recommend. Unlike survey-based WOM measures, online reviews constitute a behavioral act through which customers publicly advocate for or against a service provider by sharing evaluative experiences with others (Hennig-Thurau et al., 2004; Litvin et al., 2008). The sentiment valence expressed in review text, therefore, captures the direction and intensity of advocacy embedded in this enacted WOM behavior. Notably, prior research demonstrates that review valence significantly influences consumer decision-making and purchase behavior (Chevalier and Mayzlin, 2006; You et al., 2015). Meta-analytic evidence shows that positive eWOM increases sales, while negative eWOM decreases them (Babi´c Rosario et al., 2016). 

### _5.1. Method_ 

We employed the web data crawler Apify to collect online restaurant reviews from TripAdvisor in Sydney, Australia. Our focus was on reviews posted in 2019 to eliminate potential biases introduced by the COVID− 19 pandemic and associated lockdowns. In total, we scraped 49,902 reviews, including textual data and information on the restaurant’s price levels (measured on a 1-4 scale: 1 = $, 2 = $$, 3 = $$$, 4 = $ $$$), with no identifiers. We removed reviews written in languages other than English, resulting in a sample of 46,434 reviews. Following Pennebaker et al.'s (2015) recommendation to include only text samples with 10 or more words, we decided to analyze reviews with at least 15 words ( _M_ = 72.64, _SD_ = 68.42) to ensure they captured the restaurant customer experience adequately. This criterion resulted in 46,369 usable reviews. 

We analyzed these reviews using the Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count (LIWC) software to capture two constructs: service failure severity and CP (Pennebaker et al., 2015). To do this, we developed a custom dictionary tailored to our context. The dictionary development followed three steps. First, we created initial word lists for each construct by drawing on terms from academic literature and customer descriptions. This step produced dictionaries containing 12 words for severity and 14 for CP. Second, a research assistant and two academics validated the dictionaries for content. Based on their input, the final dictionaries contained 10 words each for severity and CP. The severity dictionary included terms indicative of strongly negative service experiences (e.g., disappoint*, unacceptable, ruined*). The CP dictionary included terms reflecting active, solution-oriented engagement (e.g., suggest*, ask*, offered). These dictionaries were then imported into LIWC for data analysis. In the third step, two researchers manually coded a random sample of 100 reviews. They rated the reviews for service failure severity and CP on a 1–5 scale. The interrater reliability ( _ICC_ Severity = 0.98; _ICC_ CP = 0.96) was acceptable (Cicchetti, 1994). Intercoder agreement helps to establish reliability by determining whether different judges consistently assign the same ratings (Lombard et al., 2004). ### **6. Study 3: Validating the mediating role of CP through experimental data** 

The aim of Study 3 is to validate results from Studies 1 and 2 and provide further support for Hypotheses 1 and 2 by determining the mediating role of CP in service recovery in the relationship between service failure severity and service-related outcomes in a controlled experiment. In this and subsequent experiments, CP is operationalized as customers’ willingness to engage in participatory recovery behaviors. This operationalization captures customers’ propensity to intervene during an unfolding recovery episode, which precedes enacted participation when direct observation of behavior is constrained by experimental design. 

### _6.1. Method_ 

We recruited 270 UK consumers through the Prolific research panel. Six participants failed the attention check and were excluded from the subsequent data analysis, resulting in a final dataset of 264 respondents (45% males; _M_ age = 46.96, _SD_ = 13.01). Participants were randomly assigned to a service failure severity condition (low severity: 15-minute delay; high severity: next available seating after a 2-hour delay) and were given a description of a service episode involving a mishap with a restaurant booking (see Appendix A for scenarios). 

After reading the scenario, participants rated their likelihood of participating in the service recovery process (five items adapted from Chan et al., 2010; _α_ = 0.93, _M_ = 3.84, _SD_ = 1.83), their level of satisfaction with service encounter (four items adapted from (Maxham III, 2001; Gelbrich, 2011); _α_ = 0.96, _M_ = 2.96, _SD_ = 1.63) and word-of-mouth (three items adapted from (Maxham III, 2001; Gelbrich, 2011); _α_ = 0.97, _M_ = 3.19, _SD_ = 1.60) (see Supplement File C). Participants also rated the scenario as realistic and believable, indicating that they were able to engage meaningfully with the service episode ( _M_ = 5.77, _SD_ = 1.03). All items were measured on a 7-point scale and averaged to compute variable scores. 

To assess the manipulation of service failure severity, we conducted a one-way ANOVA. Results showed a substantial difference between low severity ( _M_ Severity_Low = 2.42, SD = 1.29) and high severity conditions ( _M_ Severity_High = 6.35, SD= 0.83), _F_ (1, 262) = 864.39, _p <_ 0.001, _ηp²_ = 0.77, confirming the effectiveness of the manipulation. 

### _6.2. Results_

### **9. General discussion** 

This research reframes customer participation (CP) as a situationally triggered, control-restoration response that emerges during the recovery process. Across five studies, we demonstrate that severe service disruptions, rather than firm invitations, prompt customers to intervene when recovery stalls and customers’ sense of control is threatened. CP operates as a solution-oriented behavioral response through which customers attempt to regain influence over stalled recovery processes. This process view shifts attention from post-hoc evaluation to customer behavior during recovery, explaining when customers are more or less likely to engage in constructive participation. 

Participation is neither automatic nor uniformly beneficial. Quality signals create a conditional participation paradox: cues designed to convey competence (such as awards and certifications) attenuate the

This does not eliminate participation but reduces constructive intervention during recovery, while remaining distinct from other responses such as complaints, exit, or withdrawal. 

Employee responsiveness plays a stage-salient role by enhancing the extent to which CP translates into improved recovery outcomes once enacted, through signaling acknowledgment and action on customer input. However, responsiveness cannot compensate when quality signals have shifted customers away from direct participatory action. This asymmetry reveals that quality cues shape CP initiation while responsiveness shapes the extent to which CP translates into improved recovery outcomes. 

### _9.1. Theoretical contributions_ 

This research makes four theoretical contributions to the service recovery and customer participation (CP) literature. 

First, we reconceptualize CP in service recovery as a situationally triggered, solution-oriented behavioral response rather than as a proactive form of value co-creation or a function of stable customer traits. Prior research has largely treated CP as an invited or role-framed activity that enhances outcomes when customers are willing and prepared to participate (Dong et al., 2008; Vargo and Lusch, 2016). In contrast, we show that CP emerges during recovery when service failures threaten customers’ sense of control and recovery progress appears uncertain. Drawing on control theory (Carver and Scheier, 1981; Hui and Bateson, 1991), we conceptualize CP as a solution-oriented behavioral response through which customers seek to restore influence over the unfolding recovery process. This reframing shifts the attention of participation as a firm-invited system feature to participation as a situationally triggered, solution-oriented response enacted during service recovery. 

Second, this research identifies an explanatory gap in dominant recovery frameworks that CP addresses. Expectancy–disconfirmation (Oliver, 1980) and justice theory (e.g., Tax et al., 1998) explain how customers evaluate recovery outcomes, but they do not account for variation in customer action during recovery itself, specifically, why some customers intervene constructively while others remain passive. By positioning CP within a control-theoretic process, our framework explains this variation: failure severity activates a motivation to restore control, but whether that motivation produces participatory action depends on contextual cues such as quality signals. This positions CP as complementary to evaluative frameworks, addressing a distinct stage of the recovery process that existing theories leave unspecified. 

Third, we identify a conditional participation paradox associated with quality signals. Whereas signaling theory emphasizes the uncertainty-reducing and confidence-enhancing effects of quality cues (Spence, 1973; Kirmani and Rao, 2000), we show that in recovery contexts, strong quality signals can attenuate the likelihood that service failure severity triggers CP. By shaping role expectations and shifting perceived responsibility for recovery towards the provider, quality signals reduce customers’ marginal propensity to intervene constructively. This extends signaling theory by demonstrating that signals influence not only beliefs and evaluations, but also behavioral thresholds for participation during service breakdowns. The findings are in line with control theory to show quality signals can influence the control strategy a customer undertakes. 

Fourth, we contribute a stage salient temporal account of contextual influences in service recovery by distinguishing the roles of quality signals and employee responsiveness. We show that quality signals shape whether failure severity translates into participatory action, whereas employee responsiveness serves a diagnostic function once CP is enacted, conditioning the extent to which CP translates into improved outcomes. This sequencing reveals a critical boundary condition for responsiveness: its benefits are contingent on participation having already occurred. By unpacking this asymmetry, we advance a more 

dynamic understanding of how contextual cues jointly shape recovery processes and outcomes. Collectively, these contributions position CP as a situationally triggered, solution-oriented behavioral response embedded within recovery episodes, moving beyond static evaluative views to explain real-time customer action during high-stakes service failures. 

### _9.2. Managerial implications_ 

This research offers actionable guidance for hospitality firms seeking to design recovery systems that are not only operationally effective but also psychologically aligned with customer behavior during severe service failures. Across our studies, customer participation (CP) emerges as a situational response that can improve recovery outcomes; yet whether customers participate and whether participation helps depends on cues embedded in the recovery environment. 

First, firms should recognize that customers facing high-severity failures may be willing to participate in recovery, particularly when progress appears stalled, and the experience matters. However, this willingness depends on whether the context signals that participation is welcome and appropriate. Hospitality firms can facilitate constructive CP by creating visible, low-effort avenues for input at key touchpoints (e.g., in-room tablets, mobile prompts, or table cards) that explicitly invite guidance, such as: “Help us make it right: tell us what matters most now.” 

Second, firms should be aware that quality signals can attenuate customers’ likelihood of initiating participation by shifting perceived responsibility for recovery toward the provider. While certifications, awards, and rankings build confidence, they may also lead customers to assume that the firm will handle recovery autonomously. To counter this effect, firms should balance reputational cues with participatory messaging that frames customer input as part of maintaining service excellence rather than as a challenge to it. For example, a hotel might highlight its accolades in pre-arrival communication while signaling openness during service: “We are proud of our awards, and your feedback helps us live up to them.” 

Third, employee responsiveness remains critical. Although responsiveness cannot compensate when participation does not occur, it can meaningfully amplify the benefits of CP once customers choose to engage. Training should therefore emphasize acknowledgment and visible action on customer input, not just procedural resolution. When customers see that their contributions shape recovery actions, CP is more likely to restore a sense of influence, which in turn translates into higher satisfaction and positive word-of-mouth. 

Taken together, recovery should be designed as a shared process. Firms that combine quality signaling with explicit participatory cues and responsive frontline employee behavior are better positioned to harness CP when it emerges and improve recovery outcomes following severe service failures. 

### _9.3. Limitations and future research_ 

Several limitations suggest promising research directions. First, while using real-world cues like TripAdvisor certifications, future work could test fictional, platform-specific, or digital signals to isolate effects across service contexts. Second, we establish CP as a solution-oriented behavioral response that functions as a mediator linking severity to outcomes, but we did not measure psychological mediators, such as disconfirmation or justice perceptions. These findings position CP alongside, rather than replacing, these evaluative mechanisms. Future studies should jointly model CP with fairness, disconfirmation, emotion, or trust to examine how behavioral intervention interacts with post-hoc evaluation. Third, we examined quality signals and responsiveness as key boundary conditions. Additional factors, such as prior brand experience, relationship tenure, and customer expertise, are likely to influence intervention decisions and how participation is received, thereby

Future research using field-based or longitudinal designs could examine how quality signals and responsiveness jointly evolve across the recovery episode. Finally, while experiments establish causality, longitudinal field research could track CP evolution across repeated failures or extended episodes, clarifying how responsiveness shapes the extent to which participation translates into outcomes postenactment and customers’ willingness to sustain participation over time.
